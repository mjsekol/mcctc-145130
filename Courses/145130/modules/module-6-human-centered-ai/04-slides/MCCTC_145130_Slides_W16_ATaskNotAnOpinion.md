# A task, not an opinion
---
## Slide 1: Five sessions. Seventy-five minutes. One usable finding.
- Everybody finished every task
- Everybody said something kind
- One person said they did not know what "other" meant
- That sentence is the entire result
Speaker notes: A team ran five sessions last year. Seventy-five minutes of other people's time. Everybody finished, everybody was nice about it, and the whole report came down to one person saying they did not know what the word other meant. The program was fine. The script was not.

## Slide 2: A bad task destroys the measurement before you start
- The session still happens
- They still do things, you still write things down
- You come away with a full page
- And nothing you can act on
Speaker notes: This is the part that catches people. A badly written task does not announce itself. The session runs, the participant does things, your pen moves, and you leave with a full sheet of paper. It is only later, when you try to turn it into a finding, that you notice there is nothing in it.
Image: A full handwritten page with a red line through it, accent red.

## Slide 3: Three parts, every time
```
starting state   where they are, what they have in front of them
goal             what they want to end up knowing or having
reason           why a person would actually want that
```
Speaker notes: Three parts. Where they are, what they want, and why a person would want it. The reason is the one people leave out and it is the one that makes somebody try properly. Find out how many are about the network is a chore. You are covering the desk Monday and you want to know before you start is a thing somebody leans into.

## Slide 4: The same request, three ways
```
INSTRUCTION  Run it with --file notes.json, then open digest.md.
OPINION      Take a look and tell me what you think of it.
TASK         You are covering the front desk Monday and there is a
             pile of notes. You want to know how many are about the
             network before you start. Show me how you would find out.
```
Speaker notes: The instruction tests whether the steps work when somebody performs them correctly. That is a smoke test and it is a real thing, and it is not this. The opinion tests how polite they are. The task tests whether a person can start your program, find its output, and read it, which is three findings out of one task.

## Slide 5: Four things you may not say
```
1  the name of a control      "click Run", "there is a --file flag"
2  "you can"                  "you can use the arrow keys"
3  "did that work?"           tells them it was supposed to work
4  the answer                 in any form, including your face
```
Speaker notes: Write these on the inside of your clipboard. Number four includes your face, and you will make an expression when somebody goes the wrong way. Look at your paper, not at their screen. That is also why you write down times, because it gives your hands something to do while you are not helping.

## Slide 6: Four you may say, and the first one is silence
- Nothing. Silence is the default
- "What are you trying to do right now?"
- "What did you expect to happen?"
- "Take your time, I am not timing you against anybody"
Speaker notes: Silence is the default and it is correct far more often than it feels. When you do need to say something, ask what they are trying to do, not whether it worked. The last one is a small lie of omission, because you are timing. You are not comparing them to anybody, which is the part they are worried about.

## Slide 7: Six drafted tasks. Four of them are broken.
- Names the control, names the file
- Asks what they think
- Asks for something the program cannot do
- Contains a finding: "your note got put in the wrong category"
Speaker notes: In build one you get six tasks and four are broken in a named way. The one people miss is the third: a task asking for something the program does not do at all. Everybody reads it and it sounds reasonable. Somebody has to actually try it, which is tomorrow's lesson.
Image: Six task cards, four stamped with a small accent red mark.

## Slide 8: The closing question, and it is not "did you like it"
- Ask: "if you did that again tomorrow, what would you do differently?"
- It asks about behaviour, not feelings
- It gets you the thing they were embarrassed about
- That is usually the real finding
Speaker notes: One question at the end. If you had to do that again tomorrow, what would you do differently. It gets you an answer like I would go straight to the terminal, which you can act on. Did you like it gets you a compliment. Any suggestions gets you a feature request from somebody who used it for eleven minutes.
Image: A single question card, with three crossed-out alternatives beneath it in muted grey.

## Slide 9: Users and stakeholders are not the same people
- You watch users. You ask stakeholders
- Users: the people who do the work
- Stakeholders: the people who decide, pay, or own the problem
- Asking a user for an opinion is how the script goes wrong
Speaker notes: Competency one point two point thirteen is identify stakeholders and solicit their opinions, and it is not the same as asking your participants what they think. You watch users because you want behaviour. You ask stakeholders because they own what finished means. Getting those the wrong way round is exactly the mistake on slide four.
Image: Two labelled columns, one headed watch and one headed ask, with different icons.

## Slide 10: What you are about to build
- Judge six drafted tasks, name what is wrong with four
- Write four tasks for your own program
- A one-page script with the consent line at the top
- One page, or it will not run in fifteen minutes
Speaker notes: Build one is the lab, judging six tasks and then writing four of your own. Build two is the whole script: consent line word for word, your four tasks, the facilitator rules, and the closing question. One page. If it will not fit on one page it will not fit in fifteen minutes, and tomorrow you find out the hard way.

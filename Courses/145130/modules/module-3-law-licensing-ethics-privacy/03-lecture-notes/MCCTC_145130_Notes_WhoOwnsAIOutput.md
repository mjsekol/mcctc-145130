## Why this exists

Every one of you will put model output into something that matters. A capstone, a
competition entry, a portfolio, a paid job. Somebody will eventually ask you who owns it,
and the honest answer has four parts.

Here is the failure this lesson is trying to prevent. **Students, and plenty of adults,
mash four different questions into one and then argue about the mash.** The argument goes
nowhere because the four questions have four different answers, and two of them are settled
while two of them are not.

## Four questions, not one

Write these down separately. Every argument you hear about AI and copyright is one of
these four wearing the others as a disguise.

| # | The question | How settled is it |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is the **output** protected by copyright, and if so, whose | Partly settled in the United States, on one specific point, and different in other countries |
| 2 | Does the **output** infringe somebody else's work | Settled in principle, fact-specific in practice |
| 3 | Was the **training** on other people's work lawful | **Genuinely unsettled and actively being litigated** |
| 4 | What does the **model's license** let you do | Fully settled. Open the file and read it |

### Question 1: is the output protected

**What is stated clearly by the U.S. Copyright Office:** its position is that copyright
protects material that is the product of **human authorship**. Material generated by a
machine, without sufficient human authorship, is not registrable. Where a work contains
both human-authored material and AI-generated material, the Office's guidance is that the
human contribution can be registered and the AI-generated portion is disclaimed.

Read the Office's own registration guidance rather than a summary of it. **[VERIFY]**

**What that means in practice.** Typing a prompt and accepting what comes back is the case
the guidance addresses most directly. Selecting, arranging, editing, and adding to model
output is the case where a human contribution exists and the question becomes how much and
where. **The boundary is not a number and nobody should give you one.**

**This is a United States answer.** Other countries have taken different approaches, and at
least one has a statutory provision for computer-generated works that United States law
does not have. If your work goes anywhere outside the United States, that is a question for
somebody who practises there. **[VERIFY]** anything you are told about another country's
rule.

### Question 2: does the output infringe

The output is a new file. Whether it infringes is the same question you would ask about any
new file: is it substantially similar to a protected work, in the protected expression.

Usually the answer is no, because the output is new text or a new image. Sometimes it is
yes, because a model can produce something very close to a specific work, especially when
you ask it to. **Asking a model to produce something in the style of a named living artist
is a request whose output deserves a second look.** Style itself is not what copyright
protects, and "in the style of" prompts are also where the ethics question gets loudest,
which is next week.

There is a related and separate area: **rights of publicity**, covering a person's name,
voice, and likeness. Those are largely matters of state law in the United States and they
vary. This is the area behind voice and face imitation. If your project touches a real
person's voice or face, stop and ask. **[VERIFY]** Ohio's own provision with the state
before relying on anything about it.

### Question 3: was the training lawful

**This is the unsettled one, and it is the most consequential.**

The question is whether copying protected works in order to train a model is an
infringement, or whether it is permitted, in the United States most often argued under fair
use. It is being litigated right now in multiple cases, and different courts, different
facts, and different countries are producing different answers. **I am not going to name
cases or outcomes in this file, because a case that is on appeal when I write this could be
decided differently by the time you read it, and a wrong citation in a lesson is worse than
no citation.**

Here is the strongest case on each side, stated as its own supporters would state it.

**The strongest case that training is fair use:**

Training is not reading a book to a person, it is a statistical process over an enormous
corpus, and the model that results does not store or reproduce those works. The purpose is
different in kind from the purpose of the original: nobody trains a model in order to read
the novel. Copyright has repeatedly permitted intermediate copying in service of a new,
non-substitutive purpose, including in the line of cases about indexing and search. If
every act of machine analysis required a licence from every rights holder, the practical
effect is that only the largest companies could ever build these systems, because only they
could afford the transaction costs. That is a worse outcome for competition and for the
public than the alternative.

**The strongest case that training is not fair use:**

The fourth fair use factor asks about the effect on the market for the work, and there is
now a real market: models trained on somebody's work compete directly with that person for
the same commissions. That is a market harm in the ordinary sense, not a speculative one.
The works were taken without permission or payment at a scale no individual could have
consented to, and the value of the resulting system comes substantially from exactly those
works. The argument that licensing is impractical is an argument that the taking should be
free because it was large, which is not a principle copyright has ever adopted. And the
companies doing it are not small.

**Where that leaves you.** It is unsettled. Different courts may reach different answers on
different facts, and legislatures may change the rules entirely. **The honest professional
position is to say that, to say which facts you think matter, and to design so that your
project does not depend on the answer.** That last part is the practical skill and it is
your licensing audit.

### Question 4: what the license says

This one is fully settled and it takes four minutes. Open the license.

```
7. OUTPUT. Lantern Research Collective claims no ownership in material you
   generate with the Model Weights. This Section is a statement about what
   Lantern Research Collective will and will not claim under this contract. It
   is not a statement about whether any law gives you or anyone else a copyright
   in that material.
```

That is Section 7 of the invented model license in the Study Buddy tree, and it is written
in the shape of the real ones, with the second half added to make the point out loud.

**Read that again, because this is the single most common confusion in this whole subject.**
"We claim no ownership" is a promise about what the licensor will do. **A promise not to
sue you is not a grant of copyright, and no private contract can create a copyright that
the law does not provide.**

If a model's terms say you own the output, what they are telling you is that the company
will not assert a claim against you. Whether anyone holds a copyright in that output is
Question 1, and the terms of service do not decide it.


Output:

```
7. OUTPUT. Lantern Research Collective claims no ownership in material you
   generate with the Model Weights. This Section is a statement about what
   Lantern Research Collective will and will not claim under this contract. It
   is not a statement about whether any law gives you or anyone else a copyright
   in that material.
```

Now split it into the two claims it contains:

| Claim | What kind of statement it is |
|---|---|
| The licensor will not assert ownership | A **contract** promise. Settled by reading it. |
| You hold a copyright in the output | A statement about **copyright law**. This license explicitly does not make it. Most real ones do not either, and a reader supplies it anyway. |

## Worked example 2: a provenance record you can actually keep

You cannot compute who owns something. You can record who wrote what, which is the input to
that question and is the thing you will wish you had later.

```python
# authorship.py: a provenance record for one deliverable.
# It records who wrote what. It does not decide who owns what.

SECTIONS = [
    {"name": "problem statement", "words": 240, "origin": "human"},
    {"name": "data flow diagram", "words": 0, "origin": "human"},
    {"name": "first draft of the intro", "words": 310, "origin": "model"},
    {"name": "intro after rewriting", "words": 280, "origin": "human_edit_of_model"},
    {"name": "license audit table", "words": 620, "origin": "human"},
    {"name": "poster tagline", "words": 12, "origin": "model"},
]

totals = {}
for section in SECTIONS:
    totals[section["origin"]] = totals.get(section["origin"], 0) + section["words"]

all_words = sum(totals.values())
for origin in sorted(totals):
    share = 100 * totals[origin] / all_words
    print(f"{origin:<24} {totals[origin]:>5} words  {share:5.1f}%")

print(f"\nTotal words: {all_words}")
print("Sections whose first draft came from a model:",
      sum(1 for s in SECTIONS if "model" in s["origin"]))
print("\nThis is a record of who wrote what.")
print("It is not a conclusion about who owns what. Nobody can compute that.")
```

Output:

```
human                      860 words   58.8%
human_edit_of_model        280 words   19.2%
model                      322 words   22.0%

Total words: 1462
Sections whose first draft came from a model: 3

This is a record of who wrote what.
It is not a conclusion about who owns what. Nobody can compute that.
```

**Look at the last two lines of that program, because they are the point of it.** The
percentages are real and they are not an answer. A registration question, a competition
disclosure, and a conversation with a client all want the record. None of them are settled
by the percentage.

Notice the row with zero words. The data flow diagram is human work that this program
cannot count, because the program counts words. **A metric that cannot see part of the work
will always understate that part**, and in a provenance record that understates the human
contribution specifically.

## The wrong version, and what it does instead of an error

A team writes this in their competition submission, and a model wrote the first draft of it:

> "All assets in this project are original. The images and the tagline were generated with
> AI, which means they are not copyrighted by anyone else, so we own them outright and our
> submission is fully original work."

**No error. It is grammatical, confident, and it will be read by a judge.** There are four
mistakes in three sentences.

1. **"Original" is doing two jobs.** In a competition rule it usually means the team made
   it. In copyright it means it originates with an author and has a minimal spark of
   creativity. The sentence uses the first meaning to claim the second.
2. **"Not copyrighted by anyone else" does not follow from "generated with AI."** That is
   Question 2, and it is about the particular output, not about the method.
3. **"So we own them outright" does not follow from the first two.** That is Question 1,
   and the Copyright Office's stated position runs the other way for material without
   sufficient human authorship.
4. **"Fully original work" hides the disclosure.** The team disclosed the AI use in the
   middle of a sentence whose conclusion is that there is nothing to disclose.

The honest version is shorter and stronger:

> "The tagline and the hero image were drafted with a locally hosted model and then edited
> by the team. Our provenance record lists which sections started as model output. We are
> not claiming copyright in the unedited model output. Everything else is our own work and
> every third-party component is listed in LICENSES.md with its license."

## Why the wrong version is tempting

**It answers a question the team was actually asked.** Competition forms ask "is this your
original work". The form does not have a box for "it is complicated and here are four
questions", so the team writes something that fits in the box.

**Every individual sentence is the kind of thing people say.** You have heard all four of
those claims out loud. The mistake is in the joins, not in the parts, and joins are exactly
what a language model produces smoothly.

**Saying "unsettled" feels like admitting you did not do the work.** It is the opposite. In
this course and in any professional setting, **"this is unsettled, here are the facts it
turns on, here is what we did about it" is the strongest answer available.** A confident
wrong answer is the weak one, and next week you will be graded on your ability to tell the
difference.

## Vocabulary

| Term | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Human authorship** | The requirement that copyrightable material originate with a human author. Central to the Copyright Office's stated position on AI output. |
| **Registration** | Filing a work with the Copyright Office. Separate from protection, which is automatic. |
| **Disclaimer** | Excluding material from a registration, which is what the Office's guidance describes for AI-generated portions. |
| **Fair use** | A United States doctrine weighed on four factors, including the effect on the market for the work. |
| **Infringement** | Using a protected work in a way the owner has the exclusive right to control. A question about the output, not the method. |
| **Right of publicity** | A person's right in their name, voice, and likeness. Largely state law and it varies. |
| **Terms of service** | A contract. It says what the company will and will not claim. It does not decide copyright. |
| **Provenance record** | Your written record of which parts of a deliverable came from where. |
| **Unsettled** | Not yet decided by courts or legislatures. A description of the law, not an excuse. |

### Answers

**1.** They have answered **Question 4**, what the license says. They think they answered
**Question 1**, whether a copyright exists and whose it is. What to say back:

> "That clause tells you the company will not claim it. It does not tell you the law gives
> it to you, and no contract between the two of you can."

**2.** For example:

> The poster image began as output from a locally hosted model, from a prompt written by
> our team, and was then edited by our team for about two hours. Our provenance record
> lists which parts are which. We are not claiming copyright in the unedited model output.

Three sentences. It names the tool, the human contribution, and what the team is not
claiming. **It does not resolve Question 1**, because that is not the team's to resolve, and
a submission that tried would be making a claim it cannot support.

**3.** Many good answers. One pair:

**Sharpest objection to the market-harm argument:** the fourth factor asks about harm to
the market for the work, and the harm being described is competition with the creator, which
is not the same thing. A style is not protected, and a new artist who learns by studying
existing work and then competes with those artists causes exactly the same kind of economic
harm. Copyright has never treated that as infringement, so the argument proves too much: it
would make learning from work a market harm.

**Best reply to that objection:** the comparison to a human learner breaks down on scale and
on mechanism. A human who studies an artist's work produces one competitor slowly, and the
number of such competitors is limited by how long a human life is. A model trained on that
same work produces unlimited competing output immediately, at a cost near zero, and does so
because those specific works were copied wholesale into the training process. The market
effect factor is not asking whether competition is bad. It is asking what happens to the
market for the original if this use becomes widespread, and "widespread" is not a
hypothetical here.

**What earns full credit is that neither version is a straw version.** If your objection took no
effort to answer, you did not write the sharpest one.

## Why this exists

You have spent three weeks producing findings. A finding that nobody reads has
the same value as a finding you did not make.

The person who has to act on your work is usually not a technical person, is
reading it between two other things, and will decide within the first five lines
whether to keep reading. That is not laziness. It is what a day looks like for
anyone who makes decisions.

**Writing for them is a technical skill with rules**, and the rules are different
from the rules for the lab notebook you wrote everything in.

---

## The concept in plain language

Two documents, two jobs, and students collapse them constantly.

| | **Your record** | **Your brief** |
|---|---|---|
| Reader | You, later. A reviewer checking you | A person who has to decide |
| Order | Chronological. What you did, in order | Conclusion first, then support |
| Length | As long as the work took | One page. Usually less |
| Contains | Everything, including dead ends | What changes the decision |
| Tone | Complete | Direct |

**Your record is not a draft of your brief.** They are different documents built
from the same work, and turning the first into the second is not editing, it is
rewriting.

---

## The five parts of a brief

In this order. The order is not negotiable, because it is what makes the document
survive being half read.

**1. The recommendation, in one sentence, first.**
Not background. Not method. What you think should happen. If the reader stops
after one line, they still got the thing they needed.

**2. What the situation is, in three sentences at most.**
Enough for someone who has not been living inside it. Not the history.

**3. The two or three findings that drive the recommendation.**
Not all eleven. The ones that would change the decision if they were different.
Each one quoted, labelled, and with its evidence in one line.

**4. What it costs, and what you are trading away.**
Time, money, effort, or something somebody wanted. **A recommendation with no cost
reads as naive and gets discounted**, even when it is right.

**5. What would change your recommendation.**
One sentence. It tells the reader how firm this is, which is information they need
and almost never get.

## Worked example · the same work, twice

The work: an evaluation of an AI-generated set of yearbook captions, eleven
findings, three of them serious.

### The version students write first

> **Caption Evaluation Report**
>
> **Introduction.** For this assignment I evaluated a set of forty AI-generated
> captions using the five parameters of AI output evaluation, which are validity,
> relevance, authenticity, potential bias, and hallucinations. This report
> describes my methodology and findings.
>
> **Methodology.** I first read all forty captions. I then compared each caption
> to the results sheet. I recorded my findings in a table. For each finding I
> recorded the exact claim, the parameter, why it fails, and how I know.
>
> **Findings.** Finding 1: caption 4 says the game was on Friday. The results
> sheet says Thursday. This is a Validity failure...
>
> [eight more findings]
>
> **Conclusion.** Overall, the captions were mostly accurate but there were some
> issues. I would recommend checking them before printing.

**What is wrong with it.** The recommendation is in the last eight words. A
reader with four minutes reads the introduction, learns the definition of the five
parameters they already know, reads a methodology section that tells them nothing
they can act on, and stops. The three findings that matter are buried at positions
6, 7, and 9.

**And the recommendation, when you finally reach it, is unactionable.** "Check
them" does not say which, by whom, or by when.

### The version that gets acted on

> **Do not send the sports captions to print until every caption that names a
> student is checked against the results sheet. That is twelve of the forty and
> about fifteen minutes.**
>
> Forty captions were generated from the roster and the results sheet. I checked
> all forty against the sheet. Thirty-seven are correct.
>
> **Three are not, and one of them names a student.**
>
> - Caption 12 says a named sophomore scored the winning goal in the district
>   semifinal. The results sheet lists him as a manager that week. He did not
>   play. **This is the one that cannot go to print.**
> - Caption 4 gives the wrong day for the game. Results sheet, row 9.
> - Caption 31 says "the team's first district win," which the sheet does not
>   support either way. I could not confirm it and it should come out or be
>   checked with the coach.
>
> **What this costs:** fifteen minutes and a delayed sign-off. The alternative is a
> permanent printed claim about a named student that is not true.
>
> **What would change this:** if the roster you generated from distinguishes
> managers from players and I misread it, caption 12 may be correct. I could not
> tell from the copy I have.

Same work. The reader can act after the first sentence, and every sentence after
it is support for the same decision.

## The rules that do most of the work

**Lead with the decision, not the process.** Nobody is grading your methodology
here. Put it at the end, or in the record you link to.

**Name the person and the action.** "Somebody should check these" and "the
yearbook adviser should check the twelve captions that name students before
sign-off" are different documents.

**Quantify the cost.** Fifteen minutes. Twelve captions. One delayed sign-off.
Numbers stop a recommendation from reading as an abstraction.

**One recommendation.** If you have three, you have three briefs, or you have one
brief and a list of everything else at the bottom under a heading that says so.

**Say what you are not sure about.** The third bullet above says "I could not
confirm it." That sentence buys more trust than the two confident bullets, because
it shows the confident ones were a choice.

**Cut the definitions.** Your reader does not need the five parameters explained.
If they do, that is a different document, delivered before this one.

## The wrong version, and why it is tempting

Beyond the report shape above, there is one more failure worth naming: **hedging
the recommendation so that nobody can disagree with it.**

> "There may be some issues with a few of the captions, and it might be worth
> considering whether some additional review could be appropriate before print."

Nothing in that sentence can be wrong. That is why it was written, and it is why
it is useless. The reader cannot act on it, and if the caption goes to print and
is wrong, the writer can say they raised it.

**Why it is tempting.** A definite recommendation can be wrong, in front of
people, in writing. Hedging removes that risk by removing the content.

**The professional move is the opposite:** say the thing definitely, and say what
would change it. Part 5 of the brief is what lets you be direct without
overclaiming. It is not a hedge, because it names a specific fact rather than
softening the whole document.

## Adjusting for the audience

The same finding, three readers:

| Reader | What they need first | What to cut |
|---|---|---|
| **The yearbook adviser** | Which captions, by when | Anything about how models work |
| **The student who wrote them** | What to change, and that thirty-seven were fine | The print deadline framing |
| **The principal** | Whether a student's name is at risk, and what is being done | The parameter names, the method, the other two findings |

**Same evidence, three documents.** Changing the audience is not a matter of tone.
It changes what goes in paragraph one and what comes out entirely.

## Vocabulary

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **Brief** | A short document written so a decision can be made from it |
| **Record** | The complete chronological account of what you did |
| **Lead** | The first sentence, carrying the recommendation |
| **Actionable** | Names a person, an action, and a time |
| **Hedging** | Softening a claim until it cannot be wrong or useful |
| **Audience** | The specific person reading, and what they have to do next |

## Self-check

**1.** Why does the recommendation go first, when you did the work in the other
order?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because the reader stops before the end, and the order you did the work in is
about you rather than about them.

There is a second reason that matters more: writing the recommendation first
forces you to have one. Plenty of reports end in "there were some issues" because
the writer reached the end without deciding anything, and the chronological order
let them get away with it.
</details>

**2.** Your brief has three recommendations. What is the problem, and what are
your two options?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**The problem:** a reader with one decision to make now has to work out which of
your three is the one, and a document that makes the reader prioritise usually
gets none of it done.

**Option 1:** pick the one that matters most, lead with it, and put the other two
at the bottom under "also worth doing." **Option 2:** three briefs, if the three
go to different people or different decisions.

Option 1 is nearly always right for a one-page brief.
</details>

**3.** What does part 5, "what would change my recommendation," do for the reader
that a confidence level like "I am fairly sure" does not?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

It names a specific, checkable fact. "Fairly sure" tells the reader how you feel.
"If the roster distinguishes managers from players, caption 12 may be correct"
tells them exactly what to go and look at, and they may be able to settle it in
thirty seconds when you could not.

It also makes your reasoning auditable. Somebody who disagrees now knows where to
push, which is the same idea as step 6 of the scenario method and the same idea as
a bias test that could come out either way. **A claim that cannot be wrong cannot
be checked**, and that sentence has now appeared in three different lessons in
this module, which is not an accident.
</details>

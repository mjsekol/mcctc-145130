# Arguing Honestly About AI, With Somebody Unlike You
---
## Slide 1: Your paper argues the side you disagree with
- Not a trick. Not an exercise in being fake
- The fastest way to find out whether you understood
- Or whether you picked a team
- And you will use it every year after this
Speaker notes: This is the assignment people push back on hardest and value most afterwards. An argument that only works on people who already agree with you is not an argument, it is a flag. By Friday you will know which one you have been carrying.

## Slide 2: Almost every row has a real gain and a real loss
- Work, access to expertise, education, allocation
- Information environment, accessibility, energy, concentration
- The gain and the loss land on different people
- That is what makes these questions hard
Speaker notes: Most bad arguments about AI are bad because they notice one row and treat it as the whole table. AI is good and AI is bad are both statements that fail to say anything, because they do not name who gains and who loses.
Image: An eight-row table with gain and loss columns, different faces in each.

## Slide 3: Straw man and steel man
- Straw: the weakest version, held by nobody
- Steel: the strongest version, stated at full force
- The test: show it to somebody who holds it
- If they say you got it wrong, you did
Speaker notes: Refuting the weak version accomplishes nothing and it is the single most common failure in student position papers. The steel man test is uncomfortable on purpose, because comfort is what produced the straw man in the first place.
Image: Two figures, one made of straw, one made of steel, same label.

## Slide 4: Version A, written when you know the answer
```
Schools should not use AI writing detectors. People who want
them only care about catching students and do not care about
learning. Anyone who has used one knows they are terrible.
```
Speaker notes: Read the second sentence. It describes the other side's motives, standing exactly where a reason should be. That is the shape of a straw man and it is what your own first draft is going to look like.

## Slide 5: Version B, same conclusion, different paper
```
Version A: 2 of 6 parts present
Version B: 6 of 6 parts present

   states a claim · gives a reason · points at evidence
   names who is affected · states the other side · answers it
```
Speaker notes: Same position. Version B states the case for detectors in a way a teacher who wants one would recognize as their own view, and then answers it. That is the paragraph that can change somebody's mind. And do not trust the counter: you could get six of six by sprinkling phrases into Version A without changing one idea.

## Slide 6: Three questions where both sides are real
- Detectors on student work
- AI screening job applicants
- Local models at higher cost versus a cloud service
- This course chose the third and told you why
Speaker notes: For the third one, this course chose local because commercial developer APIs require users to be eighteen or older. That is a constraint-driven decision and not a claim that the argument is settled. You should be able to argue the other side of it by Friday.

## Slide 7: Your reader is not standing where you are
- US privacy framing is largely sectoral
- EU framing under the GDPR is rights based
- "There is no law against it" is strong in one
- And weak in the other
Speaker notes: Same argument, two rooms, two receptions. In a sectoral system the absence of a rule for your sector means something. In a rights-based system a general principle applies whether or not a rule names you. Neither framing is foolish and the failure is not knowing which one your reader is in.
Image: Two rooms, the same slide on both screens, different reactions.

## Slide 8: Four things to ask, never to assume
- How direct does this reader want you to be
- How much context goes into the words
- Who is authorized to disagree in this room
- What counts as evidence here
Speaker notes: These are questions about the person in front of you, never predictions about them. People from somewhere are indirect is a stereotype and it will make you wrong about the individual. I do not know whether this reader wants the conclusion first or last, so I will ask, is intercultural communication.

## Slide 9: The paragraph that says nothing
- Supporters point to personalized learning
- Critics raise concerns about over-reliance
- The truth likely lies somewhere in the middle
- Balance innovation with responsibility going forward
Speaker notes: Nobody is named. Concerns and downsides are the shape of an argument with the content removed. The truth lies in the middle is asserted, not argued. And the last clause is compatible with every possible policy, which means it recommends none. It cannot be wrong, which is why it is worthless.
Image: A paragraph in grey with every specific word removed.
---
## Slide 10: Why that paragraph keeps getting written
- It sounds fair, and even-handedness is a real virtue
- It cannot be attacked, because it claims nothing
- It is what a model produces by default
- Your rubric exists for exactly this reason
Speaker notes: If your goal is to avoid criticism rather than to be right, that is the optimal paragraph. Notice that about yourself the next time you catch yourself writing one. Your position paper is graded partly on not doing this.
Image: A shield made of hedge words.
---
## Slide 11: What you are about to build
- Pick the side you disagree with
- Write five sentences with no motive claims in them
- Name your audience and one assumption they hold
- State the evidence that would change your mind
Speaker notes: Build one is the steel man paragraph, read out loud to a partner who actually holds that view if one exists in the room. Build two names your audience and writes the sentence that says what would change your mind. That last sentence separates an analysis from a position, and it is worth real points.

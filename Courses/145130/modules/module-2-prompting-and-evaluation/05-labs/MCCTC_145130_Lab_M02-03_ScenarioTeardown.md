# Lab M02-03: The Scenario Teardown
## 145130 Applications of AI · Module 2 · Week 5 Friday to Week 6 Wednesday

**Gate:** 3 (open), with a written analysis. **Duration:** Week 5 Friday Build 2
(steps 1 and 2), Week 6 Monday Build 1 and Build 2 (steps 3 to 7), Week 6 Tuesday
(peer review), Week 6 Wednesday Build 1 and Build 2 (the brief). Due at the end of
Wednesday's block.
**Competencies:** PRIMARY 2.14.6 (critically analyze scenarios involving AI
usage), 1.2.5 (communicate information for an intended audience and purpose).
SUPPORTING 1.1.7 (problem-solving and critical thinking), 1.1.9 (give and receive
constructive feedback), 2.14.4.

---

## The scenario

> **This is a composite.** It was constructed for this class out of things that
> are common in school districts. **It is not a report of a real incident**, here
> or anywhere, and no part of it describes a real person. Treat it as though it
> landed on your desk.

> ### The attendance letters
>
> A district office staff member is responsible for the weekly attendance-concern
> letters that go to families. There are about ninety a week. Each one names the
> student, states how many days they have missed this term, and says what happens
> next.
>
> She has been writing them by hand from a spreadsheet export, which takes most of
> a day. This term she started using the district's locally hosted model. Nothing
> leaves the building and there is no account and no key. She pastes the week's
> export into a prompt and asks for one letter per row.
>
> It takes eleven minutes. The letters read well, better than her own, and
> families have said so.
>
> She checks five letters against the spreadsheet before sending. She picks the
> first five.
>
> **Three weeks in, two things came back.**
>
> One family called because their letter said their student had missed fourteen
> days. The real number was four. The export has two absence columns, one for the
> term and one running total for the year, and the letter used the wrong one. It
> used the wrong one on every letter where the two numbers differ, which is most
> of them.
>
> A second family called for a different reason. Their letter went to a parent the
> student no longer lives with. The export has a primary contact field that the
> office has not updated since the start of the year.

---

## What you will build

A six-step teardown, a written treatment of the contested question, and a one-page
brief for a named reader who has to act on it.

---

# PART 1 · Week 5, Friday, Build 2 · Steps 1 and 2

**Do these two before you have a conclusion.** That is the whole reason they are
on Friday and the rest is on Monday. A conclusion formed on Friday will bend
Monday's facts to fit it.

**Step 1.** Write what happened, in one paragraph. **Facts only.**

No motives. No adjectives. If the scenario does not say somebody knew something,
you do not get to say they knew it. If the scenario does not say she was careless,
that word does not appear.

**Result you should see:** a paragraph you would be comfortable reading aloud to
the staff member in the scenario. If you would not be, you have put a judgement in
it. Take it out.

**Step 2.** List what you were not told. **Every gap that would change your
answer.**

Aim for at least seven. Then mark the one you think matters most and write one
sentence saying why.

**Result:** a list where at least two items are things you would not have thought
of if you had not been forced to make a list. That is what the step is for.

### Acceptance criteria, Part 1

- [ ] Step 1 is one paragraph, facts only, no motives and no adjectives
- [ ] Step 2 lists at least seven gaps
- [ ] One gap marked as most important, with a reason
- [ ] **No conclusion anywhere in the file yet**
- [ ] Committed

---

# PART 2 · Week 6, Monday · Steps 3 to 6

**Step 3.** Name who is affected. **At least five, and at least one who is not
mentioned in the scenario.**

For each: one sentence on how they are affected. Not everybody is affected the
same way and not everybody is affected badly.

**Result:** a list where the people who are not in the scenario are the ones you
had to think hardest about.

**Step 4.** Name the decision point.

Somewhere there was a moment where a different choice produces a different
outcome. Write the sentence: **"A different choice here would have produced a
different outcome."** Then name it exactly.

**It is usually earlier than people think and it is usually not the moment of
using the tool.** There may be more than one. Pick the one you would change first
and say why that one.

**Result:** one named moment, with a reason for choosing it over the others.

**Step 5.** What should have happened, and what it costs.

**A recommendation with no cost is not a recommendation.** Time, money, effort,
inconvenience, or a result somebody wanted and now does not get. Quantify it where
you can: minutes, letters, people.

Then answer the question that makes this step honest: **why did the thing you are
recommending not happen already?** If your answer is "she should have thought of
it," go back. Something made the right thing expensive, and naming that is the
work.

**Result:** a recommendation, a cost with a number in it, and a sentence about why
it was not already being done.

**Step 6.** What would change your answer.

One fact. **Specific enough that somebody could go and find out whether it is
true.** Then say which way it would flip your conclusion.

**If you cannot name one, you have an opinion rather than an analysis.** Go back to
step 5 and find the assumption it is resting on.

**Result:** one checkable fact and the direction it moves you.

### Acceptance criteria, Part 2

- [ ] At least five affected parties, at least one not in the scenario
- [ ] One decision point, named exactly, with a reason for choosing it
- [ ] A recommendation with a quantified cost and an account of why it was not
      already happening
- [ ] Step 6 names one checkable fact and which way it moves you
- [ ] Committed

---

# PART 3 · Week 6, Monday, Build 2 · The contested question

The scenario contains a question that does not have a right answer, and you will
be scored on whether you noticed and on how you handle it.

**Step 7.** Here it is:

> She pasted a spreadsheet of ninety students' names, attendance records, and
> family contacts into a model. **The model runs on a district machine. Nothing
> left the building. There is no account, no key, and no third party.**
>
> Was that acceptable?

Write both sides. Each at least a paragraph. **Write the strongest version of
each, not the version that is quickest to knock down.**

Then write what you would do and why, given that both are real. Then write what
would change your mind.

**A few things to think about, and you do not have to use any of them:** whether
"nothing left the building" answers the whole question or only part of it; whether
a prompt log is a record and who can read it; whether the families were told; what
the alternative was and what it cost; whether the answer is different for ninety
students than for one.

**This is not a trick and there is no answer key position.** The reasoning is the
assessment. An analysis that reads as though one side is beyond argument has lost
marks even if the instructor agrees with the side you picked.

### Acceptance criteria, Part 3

- [ ] Both sides written, each at least a paragraph, each the strongest version
- [ ] A reader could not tell from the two paragraphs alone which side you are on
- [ ] Your own position, with a reason
- [ ] What would change your mind

---

# PART 4 · Week 6, Tuesday · Peer review

**Step 8.** Trade teardowns with one partner. Write **four comments** using the
three-part move: name the specific thing, say what you observed rather than what it
means about them, end on a question or a next action.

**At least one comment must be a genuine disagreement, with a "because" in it.**

**Step 9.** Write a response to each comment you received. Locate it, say what you
will do, and give a reason where you decline.

**Declining feedback with a reason is a complete response.** Declining it with a
shrug is not.

**Step 10.** Revise. **Mark each change in your file** so a reader can see which
comment produced it.

**Step 11.** A second reviewer, someone other than your partner, reads the revised
version cold and writes two comments.

### Acceptance criteria, Part 4

- [ ] Four comments given, each naming a step or a sentence
- [ ] At least one substantive disagreement with reasoning
- [ ] A response to each comment received
- [ ] At least two changes in your file traceable to specific feedback, marked
- [ ] Two comments from a second reviewer on the revised version

---

# PART 5 · Week 6, Wednesday · The brief

**Step 12.** Your reader is assigned. Find yours:

| Reader | Who they are | What they have to do |
|---|---|---|
| **A · the assistant principal** | She set this up and approved it | Decide what happens to the letters next week |
| **B · the district technology director** | Responsible for the model and what gets pasted into it | Decide whether anything changes about how the model is used |
| **C · the family who got the fourteen-days letter** | They were told something false about their own child | Decide whether to trust the next letter |
| **D · the superintendent** | Has heard a rumour and has four minutes | Decide whether this needs his attention |

**One page or less. Five parts, in this order:**

1. **The recommendation, in one sentence, first.** Naming a person, an action, and
   a time.
2. **The situation, in three sentences at most.**
3. **The two or three findings that drive the recommendation**, each with its
   evidence in one line.
4. **What it costs**, quantified.
5. **What would change the recommendation**, in one sentence.

**Result:** a document your reader could act on after the first sentence.

**Step 13.** Rewrite it for a different reader from the table. **Cut what that
reader does not need and change what comes first.**

**Result:** two documents from the same evidence that are visibly different in
their first paragraph and in what is missing.

**Step 14.** One paragraph: what you cut for the second reader, and why they did
not need it.

### Acceptance criteria, Part 5

- [ ] Brief 1 is one page or less, five parts in order
- [ ] The first sentence names a person, an action, and a time
- [ ] The cost has a number in it
- [ ] Brief 2 for a different reader, visibly different in paragraph one
- [ ] The paragraph explaining what was cut
- [ ] Committed and pushed

## If you get stuck

**"Step 1 keeps turning into step 5."**
You are writing "she should have checked more" in the middle of a paragraph that
is supposed to be facts. Cross out every sentence containing "should." What is left
is step 1.

**"I cannot find seven gaps."**
Go through the scenario one sentence at a time and ask, of each: what would I need
to know to be sure this means what I think it means? "She checks five letters" is
one sentence and it has three gaps in it.

**"The decision point has to be when she used the model."**
Maybe. Then answer this: if she had not used the model, would the wrong column have
been used? She was reading the same export by hand before. **Test your decision
point by removing it and asking whether the outcome changes.**

**"Both sides of the contested question feel the same to me."**
Then you are writing one side twice. Take the side you disagree with and find the
person it protects. There is one, and once you can name them, the paragraph writes
itself.

**"My brief is two pages and I cannot cut it."**
You are writing for a reader you have not pictured. Say out loud what your reader
has to decide by the end of the day, then delete every sentence that does not help
them decide it. It is usually half the document and it is usually the background.

## Stretch goal

Write brief C, for the family. It is the hardest one in the table and most people
avoid it.

Two things make it hard. **They are not deciding a policy, they are deciding
whether to trust you**, so a recommendation naming a person and an action is the
wrong shape for the first sentence. And they are owed something the other three
readers are not: a statement of what was wrong about their own child's letter,
first, in plain words.

Write it, then say in one paragraph what you had to change about the five-part
structure and why. **If you conclude that the five-part structure does not fit this
reader, say that and defend it.** That is a legitimate finding about the format.

## EXTENDED

Everything in STANDARD, plus two additions.

**Added requirement 1.** The scenario contains **two** failures, the wrong column
and the wrong parent, and they have different causes. One is a reading error inside
the generated text. The other was already in the data before the model saw it.

Analyse them separately. **Then answer the question that matters:** would your
recommendation from step 5 have caught both? If it would not, say so and say what a
recommendation that caught both would cost.

**Hint, not the answer.** Ask yourself what a person checking a letter against the
spreadsheet would see in each case. In one case the letter and the spreadsheet
disagree. In the other they agree perfectly.

**Added requirement 2.** Write a third brief for a reader who is not in the table:
**next year's version of the staff member**, who will inherit whatever policy comes
out of this and was not here for any of it.

That reader needs something none of the other four do, and working out what it is
is the requirement. Write the brief, then write one paragraph naming what you added
for them and why the other four did not need it.

**Acceptance criteria:** all STANDARD criteria, the two failures analysed
separately with the coverage question answered, the third brief, and the paragraph.

## APPLIED

**For the student who says this would never happen, or asks what it has to do with
programming.**

It has everything to do with programming. You will spend a career building things
that read a column, and the column will be the wrong one, and the output will be
fluent. **The skill being assessed is the one that sits between a working program
and a correct outcome**, and no amount of test coverage supplies it.

**Changed scenario.** Use a real situation you can describe from your own
experience, where a tool produced output that somebody acted on. A gradebook
calculation, an autocorrect in a message that mattered, a scheduling app, a
recommendation that sent somebody somewhere, a form that would not accept a valid
answer. **It does not have to involve AI.** It has to involve output that was
trusted.

**Two rules.** **No real people named**, including yourself if the story is about
you. And **no story about a person in this building**, because the scenario has to
be something the room can discuss without anybody in it being the subject.

**Everything else is the same**, including all six steps, a contested question you
identify yourself, the peer review, and two briefs for two readers you name.

**The extra requirement that makes it the same lab.** Your contested question has
to be one you genuinely do not have a settled answer to. Write both sides, then
write one sentence about which side you expected to win before you wrote them and
whether that changed.

**Grading:** same scale and dimensions. Requirements Fit is judged on whether step
1 contains no judgements and step 6 names a checkable fact.

## Why this exists

Every evaluation you have written this module gets read by somebody else. Today
you learn how to make that reading useful, in both directions.

This is not a soft skill bolted onto the technical work. In this module the work
*is* judgement, and judgement improves only one way: somebody who thinks
differently tells you where your reasoning does not hold, and you can hear it.

There is a specific failure to avoid. When the work is a verdict, feedback tends
to become an argument about the verdict. Two people trade conclusions, neither
moves, and nothing gets better.

---

## The concept in plain language

**Give feedback on the reasoning and the evidence. Not on the conclusion.**

If you disagree with a conclusion, the useful thing to say is not "I disagree."
It is which step you would not have taken, and why.

| Instead of | Say |
|---|---|
| "I think this one is Validity, not Bias" | "Your evidence is about who is left out, which supports Bias. What made you consider Validity?" |
| "This is wrong" | "Step 4 assumes the sample was random. The scenario does not say it was." |
| "Good job" | "Your Hallucination finding records the exact search string. That is the part I am going to copy." |
| "You missed some" | "You have six Validity findings and nothing else. Try the two questions a terminal cannot answer on section 3." |

Every one of the right-hand versions names a specific place in the work. That is
the whole technique.

---

## The three-part move

Use it every time. It takes ninety seconds and it is worth the structure.

**1. Name the specific thing.** A section, a finding number, a sentence. "Your
third finding" is specific. "Your analysis" is not.

**2. Say what you observed, not what it means about them.** "This finding has no
'how you know' line" is an observation. "You were careless" is a conclusion about
a person and it cannot be acted on.

**3. Ask a question or offer the next action.** "What did you search for?" or
"Add the search string and this is full credit." End on the thing they can do.

**The strongest feedback in this module is a question**, because the work is
reasoning and a question makes them walk their own reasoning again. When they
find the gap themselves it stays found.

---

## Receiving it, which is the harder half

You will get feedback that is wrong, badly phrased, or both. The skill is getting
the useful part out anyway.

**1. Write it down before you respond.** Anything at all. The act of transcribing
buys you the four seconds where the defence usually happens.

**2. Separate the observation from the judgement.** "This is confusing" is a
judgement. Underneath it there is an observation: they could not follow something.
Ask which part.

**3. Ask one clarifying question before you agree or disagree.** "Which finding?"
or "What did you expect there?" You cannot act on feedback you have not located.

**4. Say what you will do, including nothing.** "I am going to add the search
string" or "I am keeping the Bias label and here is why." **Declining feedback
with a reason is a complete response.** Declining it with a shrug is not.

**5. Never argue about whether the feedback was fair.** Whether it was fair does
not change whether it was useful, and those two conversations cannot happen at
once.

## Worked example · a peer review, twice

**The work being reviewed**, one finding from a Hallucination Hunt:

> **Finding 2.** "Lindqvist, M. (2013). Structured Output and Downstream Parsing
> Failures. Midland Review of Applied Learning, 9, 34-48."
> **Parameter:** Hallucinations. **Why:** this journal does not exist.
> **How I know:** I looked and could not find it.

### The unhelpful version

> "Your second finding is weak. You should have searched better. Also I think
> this might be Validity because the author could be real."

Count the problems. "Weak" is a judgement with no observation under it. "Searched
better" names no action. The Validity suggestion is a competing verdict with no
reasoning attached. The person receiving this can do nothing except feel bad or
argue.

### The useful version

> "Finding 2: your 'how I know' says you looked and could not find it, and it
> does not say where. I cannot check it as written. What did you search, exactly,
> and in which catalog?
>
> Separately, I wondered about the author. If Lindqvist is a real person who
> writes in this area, that changes the finding, because a real author with an
> invented paper is a different thing from a fully invented citation. Did you
> check the author separately from the title?"

Three parts, twice. It names the finding, states the observation, and ends on an
action. The second paragraph raises a genuine alternative and frames it as a
question about the evidence rather than a competing verdict.

### The good response to it

> "You are right that 'I looked' is not checkable. I searched the school library
> catalog for the exact journal name in quotation marks and got zero results, and
> I will add that.
>
> I did not check the author separately and I am doing that now. If Lindqvist is
> real I will split it into two findings: the author exists, the paper does not."

Locates the feedback, says what they will do, and does not apologise for three
lines. Declining would have been equally fine with a reason attached.

## The wrong version, and why it is tempting

Two failures, and they look like opposites.

**Feedback that is only praise.** "This is really good, I could not find
anything." Sometimes true, usually not, and it is what people give when they do
not want the friction. It leaves the person with nothing, and it quietly tells
them the work is finished when it is not.

**If you genuinely found nothing, say what you checked.** "I read all eight
findings against the four-part rule and every one has a checkable 'how you know'"
is real information. "Looks good" is not.

**Feedback that is a counter-verdict.** "I think finding 3 is Bias, not
Validity." Now there are two opinions and no argument, and the conversation goes
to who is more confident.

**Why both are tempting.** Praise is comfortable and fast. A counter-verdict feels
like expertise. Neither requires you to have read carefully, which is the tell for
both.

**The fix for the second one is one word: because.** "I think finding 3 is Bias
rather than Validity, because your evidence is about who the procedure leaves out
rather than about a claim being false." Now it can be argued on the evidence, and
one of you will change your mind.

## How this is scored

Peer feedback is graded in this module, on the giving side and the receiving
side, under Written & Documentation.

| Dimension | Points | What full credit looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Specificity | 4 | Every comment names a section, finding, or sentence |
| Observation over judgement | 4 | States what is on the page, not what it says about the person |
| Actionability | 4 | Each comment ends in a question or a next action |
| Response quality | 4 | Locates each comment, says what will change, gives a reason when declining |
| One genuine disagreement | 4 | Raised at least one substantive disagreement, with reasoning |

**The last row exists on purpose.** A review with no disagreement in it is either
a perfect artifact or an unread one, and the second is far more common.

---

## Vocabulary

| Term | What it means here |
|---|---|
| **Observation** | Something on the page that both of you can see |
| **Judgement** | An evaluation of the work or the person. Needs an observation under it |
| **Actionable** | The person can do something specific in response |
| **Counter-verdict** | Replacing a conclusion with your own, without reasoning |
| **Locating feedback** | Finding the exact place in the work it refers to |

## Self-check

**1.** Rewrite this as useful feedback: "Your scenario analysis is too one-sided."

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Something like: "Steps 1 through 4 are all from the sophomore's side, and the
junior's constraints are not in any of them. Step 5 recommends checking all forty
captions without pricing the fifteen minutes. What was she working with?"

It names the steps, states what is absent, and ends with a question. "Too
one-sided" is a judgement with no location and no action.
</details>

**2.** Somebody tells you your finding is wrong, in a tone you do not like. What
are the first two things you do?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**Write it down**, and **ask which finding.**

Transcribing buys the pause. Locating it means you are now talking about a
specific claim rather than about the exchange. Both happen before you decide
whether they are right, and neither requires you to accept the tone.

Whether it was fair is a separate conversation. It may be worth having. It is not
worth having at the same time as this one.
</details>

**3.** Why does this module grade you on raising at least one genuine
disagreement?

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Because the alternative is what a peer review turns into on its own, which is two
people signing off on each other's work in four minutes.

Everything in this module is a judgement call: which parameter, what counts as
evidence, whether a scenario has one defensible answer. On judgement calls,
agreement that arrives instantly is usually evidence that nobody looked, not
evidence that the work is right.
</details>

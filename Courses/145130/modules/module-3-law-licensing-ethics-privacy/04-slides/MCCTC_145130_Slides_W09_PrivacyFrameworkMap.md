# Which Framework Governs Which Data
---
## Slide 1: The exit criterion has an error in it
- "Name which framework governs a category of data"
- Most of these do not follow the data
- They follow the holder and the relationship
- Same fact, two hands, two different answers
Speaker notes: Your module exit criterion asks which framework governs a given category of data, and the question is subtly wrong in a way that costs people real money. Today you learn to ask three questions instead of one, and the second one is the one that decides.
Image: Two identical data cards, held by two different organizations.
---
## Slide 2: Three questions, not one
- What is the information
- Who holds it, and in what relationship
- Where are the people it is about
Speaker notes: Answer all three and the framework is usually clear. Answer only the first and you will be confidently wrong, in the way the internet is confidently wrong about HIPAA every single day of the year.
Image: Three numbered gates, the second one wider than the others.
---
## Slide 3: The six on your competency list
- HIPAA: health information, in the health system
- PCI DSS: card data, by contract not by statute
- SOX: financial reporting at public companies
- ADA: disability discrimination, including accessibility
Speaker notes: Read the second one twice. PCI DSS is not a law. It is a standard published by the PCI Security Standards Council and it reaches you through contracts with card brands and processors. It is the odd one out on this list and saying so is worth a point on any assessment.
Image: Four framework cards, the PCI card marked contract rather than statute.
---
## Slide 4: The two European ones
- GDPR: personal data, rights based, across sectors
- EUDPR: the same shape, for the EU's own institutions
- GDPR turns on people in the EU, not citizenship
- Article 35 is the ancestor of your assessment
Speaker notes: If you can say EUDPR is GDPR-shaped rules for the EU's own institutions, you have the competency. And the citizenship point matters for real: a US citizen living in Berlin is not outside it, and an EU citizen living in Ohio is not automatically inside it.
Image: Two EU flags, one over a company, one over an institution.
---
## Slide 5: The one that governs you
- FERPA: education records at covered schools
- Inspect, request correction, control disclosure
- Rights transfer at 18 or postsecondary enrolment
- Most of you are at or near that line
Speaker notes: FERPA is not on this module's competency list. It appears in Module 4 under a different code. It is taught here because your assessment is about a school workflow and pretending FERPA is not the governing framework would make the whole exercise a fiction.
Image: A school record folder with a transfer-of-rights marker at 18.

## Slide 6: Routing to candidates, never to answers
```
blood pressure  | a hospital                    | HIPAA
blood pressure  | a fitness app you downloaded  | probably NOT HIPAA
card numbers    | the school store              | PCI DSS (a contract)
attendance      | a public school               | FERPA
names, emails   | a company website, EU         | GDPR
names, emails   | an EU institution             | EUDPR
```
Speaker notes: Rows one and two are the whole lesson. Identical data, two holders, two answers. If you built a router that keyed on the data alone, both rows would say HIPAA and one of them would be wrong.

## Slide 7: Read the last line of the output
```
This program routes to candidates. It decides nothing, and it is not
legal advice. Two of these rows also have state law that is not listed.
```
Speaker notes: A routing table is a starting point that is always incomplete. Card data and health data both attract state law in many places, and this table has no column for it. A tool that never admits it is incomplete trains you to believe it is complete.

## Slide 8: The table a model gives you
- HIPAA protects all medical information
- PCI DSS is a federal law
- SOX protects customer privacy
- GDPR protects all data of EU citizens
Speaker notes: Six rows, three columns, correct spelling, five rows containing something false. The PCI row is the most checkable error in the table and it will be believed by everybody in the room who has not read this file.
Image: A clean six-row compliance table with five red flags.

## Slide 9: Why a uniform table is a warning sign
- Every row short, every row parallel
- Reads as facts of a uniform kind
- One is not a law. One is not about privacy
- Two differ in a way the table has no column for
Speaker notes: These six are not facts of a uniform kind. The format flattened out exactly the distinctions that matter. And the word citizens sounds precise while being the wrong precision, which is how a plausible summary turns into a real engineering error: somebody writes a check for citizenship and the check looks at the wrong property.
Image: A uniform table beside the same content as six differently shaped cards.

## Slide 10: The habit that catches it
- Keep one primary source open beside the summary
- Every framework publishes its own material
- HHS, PCI Council, SEC, DOJ, the Commission, ED
- Six addresses, all in your lecture notes
Speaker notes: This is the whole defence and it costs one browser tab. Your notes have the six addresses, each marked verify because government pages move. Click them before you teach or quote from them.
Image: A browser with a summary on the left and a primary source on the right.

## Slide 11: What you are about to build
- Route six invented situations to candidate frameworks
- Write the question that decides each one
- Find the row where the data is identical
- Start section 4 of your privacy assessment
Speaker notes: Build one is the routing exercise, and the grade is on the deciding question rather than the framework name. Build two writes section four of your assessment: name the framework, say what it is for, and say which ones do not apply and why. Purpose level. No section numbers you are not sure of.

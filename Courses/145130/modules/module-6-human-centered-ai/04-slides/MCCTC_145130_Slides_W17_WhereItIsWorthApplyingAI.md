# Where it is worth applying, and an estimate you can defend
---
## Slide 1: "The market is growing 23 percent a year"
- I made that number up thirty seconds ago
- It sounded completely normal
- It has a decimal-looking specificity
- Nobody in this room can check it
Speaker notes: I wrote that on the board thirty seconds ago and I invented it. Nobody flinched. It sounded like something out of a real report, because a specific number with a specific period is what research sounds like. That is the same mechanism as the fabricated citations you hunted in Module Two, wearing a different hat.

## Slide 2: You will be asked whether an idea is worth doing
- In an interview, or by a manager, within two years
- The honest answer has arithmetic in it
- Almost nobody's does
- That is the whole opportunity here
Speaker notes: Somebody is going to ask you whether some idea is worth doing. Most people answer with a number they cannot defend, because it is what everybody around them does. An answer with a multiplication in it and an assumption written underneath will stand out, and it is also the only one you can stand behind when somebody pushes.

## Slide 3: The whole formula
```
minutes per task  x  times per year  =  minutes per year
                                        ------------------
                                        and then the assumption
```
Speaker notes: That is it. How long does one take, how many are there, multiply. The line underneath is not a footnote. It is the sentence that says what has to be true for the saving to be real, and it is the part that can be wrong. An estimate without it is a claim.

## Slide 4: Three signals, and a workflow needs all three
- Repeated: on a schedule or an event, not once
- Rule-shaped or text-shaped: describable, or reading and writing
- Somebody is doing it by hand right now
- The third one is the filter that does the work
Speaker notes: Three signals. The third is the one that does the most filtering. If nobody is doing it, there is no time to save and no person to time, so every number you produce will be invented. Nobody does this and they should is a proposal, and it needs a completely different argument.
Image: Three filters stacked, with most ideas falling out at the third.

## Slide 5: One workflow, costed
```
The workflow:  the front desk reads overnight notes and writes one line
               per animal on the board before the first appointment.
How long:      18 minutes. Timed once, on one morning, with a stopwatch,
               while she did it.
How often:     every weekday the practice is open. 250 days.

18 x 250 = 4500 minutes a year = 75 hours a year.
```
Speaker notes: A vet's office, a real repeated task, one measurement. Notice how much of that block is about where the eighteen came from. Timed once, on one morning, with a stopwatch, while she did it. That sentence is worth more in your report than the seventy-five is.

## Slide 6: The assumption is the estimate
```
This saving is real only if nobody re-reads the notes to check the
summary. If the front desk reads every note anyway to be sure, the
saving is the writing time, not the reading time: about 4 minutes,
and the answer becomes 16 hours a year.
```
Speaker notes: Seventy-five hours if the output is trusted. Sixteen if every row gets checked. Both numbers go in the report, because which one is real depends on whether somebody trusts the output, and you do not know that yet. And you already know how to find out, because it is a usability session and you ran five of them last week.

## Slide 7: Hours, never money
- Money needs an hourly rate for that person
- That is not yours to ask for and not yours to guess
- A guessed rate makes a wrong number look precise
- Hand them the hours. They have the rate
Speaker notes: To turn seventy-five hours into dollars you need what that person is paid, which is not yours to ask for and definitely not yours to guess. A guessed rate makes the final number look precise while being wrong by whatever factor your guess was off by. So you stop at hours and you say that they have the rate.

## Slide 8: Where interactive media actually gets used
- Sales and marketing, interactive advertising
- Education, online learning, corporate training
- Corporate communications, news, entertainment
- Your workflow might sit in none of those, and say so
Speaker notes: These eight are competency seven point one point nine and they are the same survey move: where is somebody already spending attention. For each of your three workflows, name which one it sits in. A vet's morning intake is not interactive media, and saying so is a better answer than forcing it into a box.

## Slide 9: The required section: what I did not measure
```
1  Quality. I did not test whether a model can write a usable summary.
2  The re-check. The estimate swings from 75 to 16 on that question.
3  Set-up cost. If it takes 80 hours to build, year one is a loss.
4  Whether the practice wants it. I timed one person. I never asked.
```
Speaker notes: Every analysis in this module has this section and it is worth marks. Number four is the one professionals miss. You timed a task and never asked whether it is a problem anybody cares about, and the thing that annoys them most might be something else entirely. A saving nobody asked for is a project, not a saving.

## Slide 10: What you are about to build
- An industry somebody you know has been inside, not a tech company
- Three workflows: who does it, how long, how often, how you know
- One estimate each, with the multiplication shown
- A "what I did not measure" section naming three things
Speaker notes: Build one is the industry and the three workflows. Pick somewhere somebody you can actually ask has worked. Build two is the estimates and the section you cannot skip. And the rule with no exceptions: no market size, no adoption rate, no vendor claim, anywhere in the document.

## Slide 1: According to the Halloran Institute
- Eighty four percent of mid-sized firms
- Now run a model on their own hardware
- Plausible name, plausible number
- The institute does not exist
Speaker notes: I made that organisation up for this slide. Nobody in the room blinked, and that is the lesson. It has a plausible name, a plausible subject, and a precise number, and it reads exactly like every real citation you have ever seen. That shape is what a fabricated source actually looks like.

## Slide 2: Precision is a style, not evidence
- A number reads as research
- It is boring, so nobody checks
- Checking costs effort
- Accepting costs none
Speaker notes: Three reasons a fake statistic gets through, and none of them is that people are careless. It is specific, and specificity reads as research. It is dull, so nobody has an emotional reason to argue with it. And checking takes real work while believing takes none. Assume this is happening to you constantly.
Image: A precise decimal number floating with no source attached.

## Slide 3: Traceable means three things
- The claim is specific enough to be wrong
- The support is named precisely enough to find
- The support actually says that
- All three, or it does not count
Speaker notes: Write these down. The third one is the one people skip and it is where most real errors live. Somebody cites a real document that says something narrower than the claim they attached to it. Go and read the sentence you are citing, every time.
Image: Three checkboxes, the third one unticked.
---
## Slide 4: The four kinds of support, ranked
- A run you did, strongest
- A primary source, strong
- A secondary source, weaker
- A model's answer, not support at all
Speaker notes: The bottom row is the one to internalise. A model's output is a thing to check, never a thing to cite. If a model tells you something useful, your job is to go and find where it is actually written and cite that. If you cannot find it, you have learned something important about the claim.
Image: A four rung ladder with the bottom rung broken.

## Slide 5: Untraceable
```
"Local AI models are much slower than cloud models,
 often by a factor of ten."
```
Speaker notes: Nothing here can be checked by anybody. Which models. On what hardware. Measuring what, total latency or tokens per second. Ten times which number. This sentence came out of a real student draft and the student was not lying. They had watched something slow and written it up.

## Slide 6: Traceable
```
On the build machine, with a stand-in server set to 40 tokens at
25 ms each, bench.py reported a median total latency of 1335.7 ms
and 39.3 tokens/sec for p1_short. Raw run: runs/bench-a.json.
No real model was available, so this measures the instrument.
```
Speaker notes: Longer, and anybody with the folder can repeat it. Look at the last sentence in particular. Saying what you did not measure is part of the report. A reader who finds out later that a number came from a stand-in, when the report did not say so, stops trusting everything else in it, and they are right to.

## Slide 7: When there is no command to run
- Say what would settle it
- Find the primary source
- Read the part that matters
- Write down the search that failed
Speaker notes: Some claims have no terminal command. The method is these four steps in order. Step one is the filter: if you cannot finish the sentence this would be settled by blank, the claim is not about the world and no amount of searching will help.
Image: Four numbered steps with the first one highlighted.
---
## Slide 8: A failed search is a result
- I looked in X and Y
- I did not find the origin
- That sentence belongs in your report
- It is stronger than studies show
Speaker notes: This is the part students resist because it feels like admitting defeat. It is the opposite. An honest failed search tells a reader something real and repeatable. Studies show tells them nothing at all. Leaving out the failed search to keep a paragraph tidy is the actual failure.
Image: A search box with no results, screenshotted into a report.
---
## Slide 9: Three questions before the sentence ends
- What exactly am I asserting
- What would show it
- Where is that thing
Speaker notes: This is the habit. Ask these three before you finish writing the claim, not after. If the third has no answer you have two honest choices: weaken the claim until it matches what you know, or go and get the evidence. There is no third choice and everyone knows is not one.
Image: Three questions written above a half finished sentence.

- The Infographic Autopsy starts now
- A page about how a language model works
- Constructed for this class, and it says so

Speaker notes: Build two is the autopsy and it runs into tomorrow. You did one of these as juniors on a Python cheat sheet where a terminal settled everything in eight seconds. This one is harder, it is about material you learned last week, and at least one thing on it is a real argument rather than a mistake. Finding that one and arguing it is worth more than finding three obvious ones.

# Conflicts of Interest, and the Three Rulebooks You Live Under
---
## Slide 1: Somebody is about to ask you which tool
- A teacher asks what the class should use
- A club asks which vendor to go with
- A manager asks which library we standardize on
- You have a reason they cannot see
Speaker notes: You are going to be asked for a recommendation sooner than you expect. The failure here is almost never corruption. It is a person with a real preference, a real relationship, and no place in the process where the relationship gets written down.
Image: A hallway conversation, one person asking, one person about to answer.
---
## Slide 2: What a conflict actually is
- A personal interest that could influence a decision
- Made on somebody else's behalf
- Could influence, not did influence
- Or could reasonably appear to
Speaker notes: Three parts of that sentence do the work. Could, not did: having one is not an accusation, it is a fact about a situation. Appear to: the value being protected is not your integrity, it is the decision's. And on somebody else's behalf: choosing a laptop for yourself cannot be a conflict.
Image: One person, two arrows, one pointing to a decision and one to a personal interest.
---
## Slide 3: Three rulebooks, all at once
- Personal: your values, family, community
- Organizational: policy, contract, purchasing rules
- Professional: the ACM and IEEE codes, the field
- They collide, and the collisions are real
Speaker notes: Your personal standard says be loyal to your friend. Your professional standard says report the defect in their code. Both are real obligations. When they collide, the professional standard is the one written down by people who are not in the room, and that is what makes it the one that protects you.
Image: Three overlapping circles with the overlaps shaded.
---
## Slide 4: The shapes it takes
- Personal gain, project bidding, gifts, outside work
- Using access you were given for a job
- Benchmarking or grading your own work
- Writing the criteria after seeing the products
Speaker notes: The last one is the quiet one. If the requirements list is written after somebody has already fallen in love with a tool, those requirements will describe that tool. The fix is to write the criteria first and date the file.

## Slide 6: Three rankings, one set of numbers
```
Every score:            NorthPath 253, Open Tutor 215, Kettle Ridge 207
Conflicts dropped:      Open Tutor 215, Kettle Ridge 207, NorthPath 74
Average of what is left: NorthPath 74.0, Open Tutor 71.7, Kettle Ridge 69.0
```
Speaker notes: Three different first places from one set of numbers. Dropping the conflicted scores compares a sum of three against a sum of one, which is meaningless, and the program printed it anyway. Averaging puts NorthPath back on top on a single score from a single person.

## Slide 7: Arithmetic cannot remove a conflict
- Once it is in the data, no formula gets it out
- A committee that finds out afterwards has only bad options
- Disclosures before scoring means two replacements
- And nine clean scores instead of nine unusable ones
Speaker notes: This is the lesson. Not that one ranking is right. That none of them are, and that the fix had to happen weeks earlier. Chen scored NorthPath twenty one points above the next vendor, the biggest gap in the table. That is not proof of anything, and the point is that nobody can tell any more, including Chen.
Image: Three podiums with three different winners, all greyed out.

## Slide 8: Four moves, and doing nothing is not one
- Disclose: write it down, before the decision
- Recuse: step out of that one decision
- Divest: get rid of the interest
- Decline: turn down the role entirely
Speaker notes: Disclosure is the first move in every case and often the only one needed. Notice the mechanism in all four: you hand the decision to somebody else. That is what makes the decision survivable when somebody finds out later.
Image: Four steps ascending, the first one wide and the last one narrow.
---
## Slide 9: What a real disclosure sounds like
- Name the relationship and how close it is
- Offer options
- Hand the decision to the person who owns it
- Before the decision, in writing
Speaker notes: My mother has worked at NorthPath for six years in an unrelated department. I am happy to score the other two and step out for NorthPath, or step out entirely, whichever the committee prefers. That is it. Four sentences and the decision is now trustworthy.
Image: A short written disclosure, dated, in a decision file.

## Slide 10: The three sentences that keep people quiet
- "It would not change my scoring"
- "Everybody knows my mom works there"
- "It is only a conference trip, not a bribe"
Speaker notes: The first is probably true and beside the point. The second confuses known informally with disclosed. The third is correct, and reciprocity does not require a bribe, which is exactly why gift policies have low thresholds. And notice: the thing that ends a career is almost never the conflict. It is the nondisclosure.
Image: Three quote cards, each with a short rebuttal beneath it.
---
## Slide 11: What you are about to build
- Write the evaluation criteria first, and date the file
- Collect disclosures before anybody sees a product
- Score, then compute the ranking three ways
- Write your own disclosure for the competition judge
Speaker notes: Build one runs the bid exercise in the right order, criteria first, disclosures second, scores third. Build two is personal: write the disclosure you would actually send about a judge, a sponsor, or a family connection in your own competition entry. If you genuinely have none, write the one you would send if you did.

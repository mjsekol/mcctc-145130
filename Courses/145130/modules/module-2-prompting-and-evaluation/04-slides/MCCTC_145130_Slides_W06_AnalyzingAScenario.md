# Analyzing a Scenario Involving AI
---
## Slide 1: You have an opinion in four seconds and nobody can check it
- This week the thing in front of you is a situation
- Somebody used a model and something happened
- You have to say what you think
- Good instincts with no method produce an unreviewable opinion
- In a meeting that is worth nothing
Speaker notes: For two weeks you have evaluated outputs. Today the thing in front of you is a whole situation with people in it. The exam asks this directly, and so does every job that uses these tools. It is also the place where a person with good instincts and no method produces an opinion nobody can check. A method is the difference between a verdict and an analysis, and the verdict is the least interesting part of either one.

## Slide 2: The scenario
```
A COMPOSITE, constructed for this lesson from patterns that are common in
schools. It is not a report of a real incident.

A junior on the yearbook staff is behind on captions for the sports section.
She pastes the roster into a locally hosted model along with the game results
and asks it to write one caption per photo. It produces forty captions in two
minutes. They read well. She checks six of them against the results sheet,
finds them correct, and uses all forty.

Two weeks later the yearbook goes to print. One caption says a sophomore
scored the winning goal in the district semifinal. He did not play in that
game. He was listed as a manager that week.
```
Speaker notes: Say the first line out loud every time you use this, and do not skip it. This is a composite built from patterns that are common in schools, and it is not a report of a real incident. You are being trained to detect fabrication, so if I presented an invented case as a real one I would have destroyed the premise of the course to make a point. Now read it twice. Once for what happened, once for what you were not told.

## Slide 3: Six steps, and the order is the method
- State what happened, with nothing added
- List what you were not told
- Name who is affected, including people not in the scenario
- Identify the decision point
- Say what should have happened, and what it would cost
Speaker notes: Do them in order, because steps one and two are the ones that get skipped and they are the ones that change the answer. Step one is facts only, no motives and no adjectives. If the scenario does not say somebody knew something, you do not get to say they knew it. Watch how much of what people say about this scenario is actually a conclusion smuggled into step one. She was careless. She should have known. The model failed. None of those are in the paragraph and two of them are verdicts.
Image: Six numbered steps in a column with the first two boxed and labelled the ones that get skipped.

## Slide 4: Step 6 is the whole thing
- State the one fact that would change your conclusion
- If you cannot name one, you have an opinion
- It makes your reasoning checkable by somebody who disagrees
- It tells you what to go and find out
- A claim that cannot be wrong cannot be checked
Speaker notes: A conclusion you would hold no matter what is not a conclusion about this scenario. It is a position you walked in with. Writing down the fact that would change your mind does three things. It makes your reasoning checkable by somebody who disagrees with you. It tells you what to go and find out, if you can. And it stops you defending a verdict when the facts move, which is the most expensive habit in professional judgement. This is the same shape as a testable theory in troubleshooting and the same shape as a bias test that could come out either way.

## Slide 5: Step 2, and this is where the lesson lives
```
WHAT YOU WERE NOT TOLD

1. Whether anyone else reviewed the captions before print.
2. Whether the yearbook has a fact-checking step at all.
3. Whether the roster's "manager" notation was visible in what she pasted.
4. Whether the six she checked were chosen at random or were the first six.
5. How much time she had, and whether a slower method was available.
```
Speaker notes: Take these from the room and they will give you three and stop. The fourth one is the one nobody asks and it is the one that changes the recommendation. Read it out and let it sit before you explain it.

## Slide 6: The fourth gap is the one nobody asks
- If she checked the first six, she checked the quickest six
- A spot check that is not random is not a spot check
- That single fact changes what you recommend
- Step 2 is what separates an analysis from a reaction
Speaker notes: Under time pressure the mind treats the scenario as complete and reasons from what is present. Forcing yourself to write the list produces gaps you would not have noticed, and here one of those gaps moves the whole recommendation. If the sample was not random, the problem is not that she checked too few, it is that the check produced confidence without producing evidence. It is also what makes your analysis checkable, because a reader who knows one of your gaps can tell you, and your conclusion updates instead of getting defended.

## Slide 7: Steps 3 and 4
```
STEP 3. WHO IS AFFECTED
The sophomore. A false claim about him, printed, permanent, with his name on
it. He is the person most affected and he is not in the scenario.
Also the junior, the readers who cannot tell which captions were checked,
next year's staff, and every student whose roster data went into a prompt.

STEP 4. THE DECISION POINT
Not the moment she used the model. The spot check. Six of forty, and the
manager notation is exactly the unusual case a small spot check misses.
```
Speaker notes: Step three has a rule in it. The person most affected is often the one not in the scenario, and if your list only has the people the paragraph names, go back. Step four is the one that surprises people. Using the model was reasonable, it saved real time, and the output was mostly right. A spot check on generated output is not a sample of a process that is uniformly good or bad, it is a sample of a process that is right about common cases and wrong about unusual ones. There is an earlier decision point worth naming too. The yearbook has no fact-checking step, so this was going to happen eventually. A model made it faster.

## Slide 8: Step 5 needs a price on it
- Check every caption that names a person and an action
- Forty checks, roughly fifteen minutes
- Fifteen minutes she did not have, which is why she skipped it
- A recommendation that ignores that is one nobody follows
- Cheaper version: check only the captions naming a person
Speaker notes: A recommendation with no cost is not a recommendation. Time, money, effort, inconvenience, or a result somebody wanted and does not get. Fifteen minutes is a real number and it is also the reason the right thing did not happen, so an analysis that pretends the fifteen minutes is free has not finished. Then look at the cheaper version, because this is where an analysis earns its keep. Twelve checks instead of forty gets most of the benefit for a third of the cost, and that is the version somebody will actually adopt.

## Slide 9: The wrong way, and it takes thirty seconds
- She should not have used AI. It made up a fact
- The model did not invent the student or the game
- It drew a wrong connection from real data she supplied
- No cost in it: forty captions by hand is hours
- No fact would change it, so it is a position
Speaker notes: This is what gets written first and it is partly right, which is what makes it dangerous, because it will be graded as analysis by anyone who happens to agree with it. It skips step one, because the model did not make up the student or the game. It skips step two entirely, so nobody can check it. It prices nothing. And it fails step six. The mirror image is equally wrong, and somebody in every room writes it. Thirty-nine out of forty beats a tired student at midnight, so the process worked. That one skips step three, because the sophomore is not in it, and treats an error rate as the only thing that matters when the harm lands on one named person.

## Slide 10: When the question is genuinely contested
- The strongest version of the case on one side
- The strongest version of the other, not one you can knock down
- What you would do, and why, given both are real
- What would change your mind
- Writing as though one side is settled loses marks
Speaker notes: Some scenarios do not have a right answer and you are scored on whether you noticed. The word that does the work in that list is strongest. Not the version most convenient to argue against. A scored analysis of a contested question that reads as though one side is beyond argument has lost marks, even if you picked the side I agree with. The reasoning is the assessment, and that is true of everything you write in the back half of this module.

## Slide 11: What you are about to build
- Scenario Teardown, steps three through five
- Step 3 names five affected parties, one not in the scenario
- Step 4 names one decision point, in the required sentence
- Step 5 has a cost in it, quantified
- Then step 6, and both sides of the contested question
Speaker notes: Steps one and two were done Friday. Build one is steps three through five. Step four uses the sentence exactly as the lab gives it. A different choice here would have produced a different outcome. Step five has a number in it. Build two is step six and the contested part of the scenario, written so a reader could not tell which side you believe. At close, two of you read only your step six aloud, and the room decides whether the fact you named is one somebody could go and find out. If it is not, it is not a falsifier.

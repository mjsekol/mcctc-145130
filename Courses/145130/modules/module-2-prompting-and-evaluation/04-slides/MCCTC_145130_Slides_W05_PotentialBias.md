## Slide 1: This is the parameter the class scores worst on, every year
- You already know the everyday meaning of biased
- Unfair, wrong, written by somebody with an agenda
- The competency means something narrower and more useful
- Whose perspective is built in, and who is missing
- Not is it unfair. Not is it wrong
Speaker notes: I can predict this one before the papers come in. Every year this is the lowest scoring parameter, and the reason is not that it is harder. It is that you arrive with the everyday meaning of the word and never swap it out. In competency two point fourteen point four, biased does not mean unfair and it does not mean wrong. It means something narrow, and the narrow version is what the exam assesses and what a professional review needs. Who is assumed, and who is absent.

## Slide 2: Every output describes a default person
- Read a paragraph of advice and ask who it talks to
- It assumes a ride home, a quiet place, grade-level English
- None of those assumptions is a lie
- The finding is that it treats that somebody as everybody
- A system built from it has no place for anyone else
Speaker notes: Read any paragraph of advice and you will find assumptions the text never states. That the reader has a ride home. That they have somewhere quiet. That they are a student and not a teacher or a parent or an alum. Every one of those is true of somebody. Where does it come from? The model produces the typical continuation from what it was trained on, so whoever writes the most on a topic supplies the typical perspective and whoever writes the least is absent. Nobody chose that. It is an average, and an average has no representative in the room. Your prompt is the second source, and it is closer to home.

## Slide 3: Three questions, in this order
- Who is the default person here
- Who in this building does this not fit
- Does the output acknowledge them, or is it silent
- Silence is the finding
- Read looking for absences, not for something offensive
Speaker notes: Students get stuck because they read looking for something offensive, and most outputs are not offensive, so they find nothing and write nothing. Read looking for absences instead. Question two has a rule attached. Name a real category of person and be specific. A student with a job after school. A student who takes the bus. A teacher. A parent volunteer. An English learner. A student on an IEP. Pick one you can describe. Then question three, and silence is the finding. An output that says this assumes you have time after school, which not everyone does, has done the work.

## Slide 4: The grade level line, worked through
```
THE CLAIM
"Write the borrower's grade level, not their schedule."

DEFAULT PERSON  A student. Grade level is a property students have.
WHO IT DOES NOT FIT  A teacher borrowing a camera for a class. A parent
volunteer at an event. An alum helping at a shoot.
ACKNOWLEDGED  No. The sentence is stated as the rule.
PARAMETER  Potential Bias. Nothing in the sentence is false.
```
Speaker notes: Three questions, three answers, and a label at the bottom. Read the last line twice. Nothing in the sentence is false, and that is what makes this a Bias finding rather than a Validity one. If you catch yourself reaching for the word wrong, you have changed parameters without noticing.

## Slide 5: What makes it a real finding
- A sheet built from this has a column only students can fill
- People who cannot fill it are turned away or recorded wrongly
- The fix: ask who is borrowing and how to reach them
- Grade level is one way to answer that, for one group
Speaker notes: The consequence is the part students leave out, and it is the part that makes a finding actionable. A checkout sheet built from that sentence has a column that only students can fill, so the teacher borrowing a camera either gets turned away or gets written down wrongly. Now look at the fix, because a fix is what proves you understood the finding. The field is not grade level. The field is who is borrowing this and how do we reach them, and grade level is one answer to that question for one group of people.

## Slide 6: An output with no bias finding
```
THE CLAIM
"Photograph any damage before the item leaves the room."

DEFAULT PERSON  Anyone handling equipment.
WHO IT DOES NOT FIT  It requires a camera, which everyone in the room has.
ACKNOWLEDGED  Nothing to acknowledge.
FINDING  None. "I looked for a Potential Bias failure in the procedure
section and did not find one."
```
Speaker notes: Run the same three questions and land on nothing, out loud, deliberately. That last line is a finding and it gets written down, because it tells a reader that you checked. Now the harder half of this. Manufacturing a finding to fill the category is its own failure mode, and reviewers do it under pressure to look thorough. It costs you credibility, because the reader who checks your weakest finding is the one who decides how much to trust your strongest one.

## Slide 7: The wrong way to write a bias finding
- Written first: biased because it only mentions American schools
- It might be a real finding. As written it is not
- It never names who is harmed by the absence
- It never says why that matters here
- Nobody can fix anything based on it
Speaker notes: Read that sentence again. It is true, it is fast, and it sounds like analysis, which is exactly why it gets written. And it is unactionable. Nobody can change a paper based on it, and unactionable is the test for whether you have written a finding or an observation wearing a finding's clothes. There is one more wrong version worth naming out loud. Deciding an output is biased because you disagree with its conclusion. Bias is about what is absent, not about whether you like what is present. If you only detect bias in outputs you disagree with, you have found a bias in your review process, and that is the finding.

## Slide 8: The same observation, written as a finding
```
Every example is a US public high school with a 7-period day. Our exchange
students and any reader outside the US get advice built on a schedule they do
not have, and the output never says the schedule is an assumption.
```
Speaker notes: Same observation as the last slide. This version names the absent group, says what goes wrong for them, and identifies the unstated assumption. Three parts, and it is the third one that most people miss. The output never says the schedule is an assumption. That is the silence, and the silence is the finding. Read it and count the three parts with the room before you move on.
Image: None. This slide is a finding.

## Slide 9: Test for it rather than arguing about it
- An opinion about bias is hard to settle. A test is not
- Keep the prompt identical, vary one detail about the person
- Record every output, compare on something countable first
- Against the stub all three come back identical. Say so
- A test that can only come out one way is nothing
Speaker notes: Three prompts. A student falling behind. A student with an after-school job falling behind. A student who takes the bus falling behind. Everything else identical, word for word. Against our stub those come back with the same reply words, the same shape and the same hedge count, because the stub is a caricature and does not model this. Write that down as an honest negative result about the stub, and say clearly that it is not evidence about a real model. Two hard rules for any bias test in this course. No real people as subjects, and no student data in any prompt. Invented people only.
Image: Three prompts differing by one clause, feeding three outputs laid out for comparison.

## Slide 10: Bias is not Validity, and the middle case is the argument
- This statement is false: Validity
- This statement is not true for everyone: usually Validity
- It assumes a kind of person and is silent about others: Bias
- Overstating a true claim is fixed by qualifying the claim
- A bias finding is fixed by naming who the advice is for
Speaker notes: The middle row is where most of the class lands and it is the one to argue about out loud. Handwritten notes help you remember more, stated too broadly, is an overstatement and that is a Validity problem, and you fix it by qualifying the claim. The Bias version of the same sentence is a different thing. It assumes a reader who can write by hand, who has notes to take, and who is in a class where note-taking is the mode, and it never says so. That one is not fixed by qualifying. It is fixed by acknowledging who the advice is for.

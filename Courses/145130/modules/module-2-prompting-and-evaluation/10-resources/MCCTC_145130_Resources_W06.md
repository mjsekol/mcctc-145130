## 1. Primary reading

`../03-lecture-notes/MCCTC_145130_Notes_AnalyzingAnAIScenario.md`

**Why this one.** It works one scenario all the way through all six steps, in order, and then shows
the wrong version next to it. The wrong version is the one you will write first, so read both.

The six steps: what happened with nothing added, what you were not told, who is affected including
people nobody mentioned, the decision point, what should have happened and what it costs, and what
would change your answer.

**Step 6 is the whole thing.** A conclusion with no falsifier is a position you brought into the room
with you. The section called "Why step 6 is the whole thing" is the part to read twice.

**Assign a question, not the page:** *Read step 2 in the worked example. The class reliably produces
three gaps and stops. What is the fourth gap, and why does it change the recommendation rather than
adding detail to it?*

Read the section on presenting a contested question before Monday Build 2. You will be asked to write
both sides so well that a reader cannot tell which one you believe.

**Time.** 25 minutes. **Level.** On-level.

## 2. Feedback on reasoning

`../03-lecture-notes/MCCTC_145130_Notes_FeedbackOnReasoning.md`

**Why this one.** It gives the three-part move and then shows the same peer review twice, unhelpful
and useful, so you can see the difference rather than being told about it.

The move: name the specific thing, say what you observed rather than what it means about the person,
end on a question or the next action.

**Receiving it is the harder half**, and the notes give that its own section. Write it down, locate
it, ask one clarifying question, say what you will do including nothing, and never argue about whether
the feedback was fair.

**The failure to avoid:** feedback on the conclusion. "I think finding 3 is Bias, not Validity" gives
the receiver two options, agree or argue, and neither improves the work. Add "because" and the
evidence and it becomes usable.

**Assign a question:** *Self-check question 1. Rewrite "your scenario analysis is too one-sided" as
useful feedback. Name the place, state the observation, end on an action.*

**Time.** 20 minutes. **Level.** On-level.

## 3. Writing for the person who has to act

`../03-lecture-notes/MCCTC_145130_Notes_WritingForTheDecider.md`

**Why this one.** It puts the two versions of the same report side by side: the version students write
first, and the version that gets acted on. Reading only the first four lines of each is the whole
lesson, because the first four lines are what the reader gets.

Five parts in order: the recommendation in one sentence, the situation in three, the two or three
findings that drive it, what it costs, and what would change it.

**Your record and your brief are different documents built from the same work.** A brief that tries to
be the record is the most common failure on Wednesday, and it reads as thoroughness to the person who
wrote it.

**Assign a question:** *Self-check question 2. Your brief has three recommendations. What is the
problem with that, and what are your options?*

**Time.** 25 minutes. **Level.** On-level.

## 8. Plain language guidance

`https://plainlanguage.gov/` · **[VERIFY]** before assigning.

**Why this one.** It is public writing guidance produced for United States federal agencies, and its
subject is the exact problem Wednesday sets: how to write so that the person who has to act can act.
It is free, it is not selling anything, and its examples are before-and-after pairs rather than rules
in the abstract.

**What to take from it and what to leave.** Take the guidance on putting the main message first, on
short sentences, and on naming the actor in a sentence. Leave anything specific to federal regulatory
documents, which you are not writing.

**Assign a question:** *Find its guidance on where the main message goes. Then open your own brief and
count how many words a reader gets through before they reach your recommendation.* Wednesday's
deliberate error is a recommendation buried in paragraph four.

**Time.** 25 minutes. **Level.** On-level.

## 9. Two writing guides, named with no URLs

**Search each by its exact name.** **[VERIFY]** whatever you find.

- **The 18F Content Guide**, a public content style guide written for United States government
  digital teams. It may have been moved or archived since it was written, so check its current status
  before you assign it, and say to the class if it has moved.
- **The Nielsen Norman Group** writing on the inverted pyramid and on how people read on screen. Their
  site root is `https://www.nngroup.com/` · **[VERIFY]**, and the specific article path is
  **[VERIFY]** as well. Search their site for "inverted pyramid".

**Why these.** The inverted pyramid is the newsroom convention behind the five parts of a brief: the
conclusion first, then the support, then the detail somebody can stop reading. Seeing that the
convention predates you, and predates these models, is the point. Wednesday is not teaching a course
format. It is teaching a hundred-year-old professional habit.

**The honest caveat.** Both of these are about writing for readers on a screen, not about AI. Nothing
in this list is a current article about how organizations write up an AI incident, because no such
article is linked here that could be confirmed live. Resources 10 and 11 are the industry connection
instead, and both are named rather than linked for the same reason.

**Time.** 30 minutes. **Level.** Extension.

## 10. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework

**Named with no direct URL. Search for it by that exact title.** The publisher is the United States
National Institute of Standards and Technology. **[VERIFY]** before assigning, including which version
is current.

**Why this one.** It is the closest thing to a shared professional vocabulary for the question Monday
asks, which is how an organization decides whether an AI use is acceptable. Its structure is a small
set of functions covering how an organization governs its AI use, maps the context, measures what it
can, and manages what is left.

**What a student should take from it.** Not the framework itself, which is written for organizations
and is long. Take the idea that "is this AI use acceptable" is a question people answer with a
documented process rather than an opinion, and that the process starts with context rather than with
the technology. That is step 1 and step 2 of Monday's method, written by adults for money.

**Assign a question:** *Find the part of the framework that deals with context before measurement. Say
in two sentences how it lines up with steps 1 and 2 of Monday's six-step method, and name one thing it
asks for that the six steps do not.*

**Time.** 40 minutes at skim depth. **Level.** Extension.

## 11. The AI Incident Database

**Named with no URL. Search for it by that exact title.** **[VERIFY]** before assigning.

**Why this one.** It collects reports of harms involving deployed AI systems. It is the closest
available source of real scenarios, and Monday's method was built for exactly this shape of material.

**Read it the way this module taught you to read anything.** Entries are submitted reports of varying
quality and varying completeness. **Each entry is a claim, not a finding.** Before you use one in your
own writing, run step 2 on it and write down what you were not told. If you cannot establish the facts
well enough to defend a conclusion, that is a result worth recording, and it is a more honest write-up
than one that treats a database entry as settled.

**Assign a question:** *Pick one entry. Write step 1, the facts with nothing added, in one paragraph
with no motives and no adjectives. Then write step 2. How many of your gaps could the entry itself
have closed, and how many need a source it does not give you?*

**Time.** 30 minutes. **Level.** Extension.

## 14. A free video

**[VERIFY] No specific video is linked here, and naming the gap is the correct answer.** There is a
large amount of free video on executive summaries and business writing, and most of it is either a

**Search for a video by this description:** somebody putting a long document and a one-page brief side

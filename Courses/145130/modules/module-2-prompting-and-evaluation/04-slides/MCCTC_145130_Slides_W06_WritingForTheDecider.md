# Writing for the Person Who Has to Act
---
## Slide 1: Three weeks of work and the reader stopped at line five
- The person who acts on your work is not technical
- They are reading it between two other things
- They decide in five lines whether to keep reading
- That is not laziness. That is what their day looks like
- A finding nobody reads is a finding you did not make
Speaker notes: You have spent three weeks producing findings. Today is about whether any of them reach a person who can do something. The yearbook adviser, the club advisor, an assistant principal. They are not technical, they are reading your document between two other things, and they will decide inside the first five lines whether to keep going. Do not take that personally and do not call it laziness. It is what a day looks like for anybody who makes decisions, and writing for them is a technical skill with rules.

## Slide 2: Two documents, two jobs
- Your record: chronological, complete, including dead ends
- Your brief: conclusion first, one page, what changes the decision
- Your record is written for you, later, and for a checker
- Your brief is written for a person who has to decide
- Your record is not a draft of your brief
Speaker notes: Students collapse these two constantly and it is the single biggest reason a good analysis lands with nobody. The record is chronological and as long as the work took, and it includes the dead ends, because a reviewer checking you needs the dead ends. The brief is conclusion first and it contains only what changes the decision. Turning the first into the second is not editing. It is rewriting, and you should expect it to take real time.
Image: Two documents side by side, one thick and chronological, one a single page led by a bold sentence.

## Slide 3: Five parts, and the order is not negotiable
- The recommendation, in one sentence, first
- The situation, in three sentences at most
- The two or three findings that drive the recommendation
- What it costs, and what you are trading away
- What would change your recommendation
Speaker notes: The order is what makes the document survive being half read. Part one first means that if the reader stops after one line, they still got the thing they needed. Part three is two or three findings, not all eleven, and the test is which ones would change the decision if they were different. Part four has a number in it, because a recommendation with no cost reads as naive and gets discounted even when it is right. Part five tells the reader how firm this is, which is information they need and almost never get.

## Slide 4: The wrong way, and it is what gets handed in first
```
CAPTION EVALUATION REPORT

Introduction. For this assignment I evaluated a set of forty AI-generated
captions using the five parameters of AI output evaluation, which are
validity, relevance, authenticity, potential bias, and hallucinations. This
report describes my methodology and findings.

Methodology. I first read all forty captions. I then compared each caption to
the results sheet. I recorded my findings in a table.

Findings. Finding 1: caption 4 says the game was on Friday. The results sheet
says Thursday. This is a Validity failure...

Conclusion. Overall, the captions were mostly accurate but there were some
issues. I would recommend checking them before printing.
```
Speaker notes: Read the introduction and the methodology aloud and then stop, because that is where the reader stops. Ask the room what they have learned that they could act on. Nothing. The work behind this document may be excellent. None of it arrived.

## Slide 5: What the reader actually got
- The recommendation is in the last eight words
- They read a definition of five parameters they already know
- They read a methodology they cannot act on, and stopped
- The findings that matter sit at positions six, seven, nine
- Check them does not say which, by whom, or by when
Speaker notes: Four minutes of reader attention spent on a definition they did not need and a methodology nobody is grading. And when you finally reach the recommendation, it is unactionable. Check them before printing names no captions, no person, and no deadline. Somebody who wanted to do the right thing still could not, from that document. Those are two separate failures and you can commit either one on its own.

## Slide 6: The version that gets acted on, first three lines
```
Do not send the sports captions to print until every caption that names a
student is checked against the results sheet. That is twelve of the forty and
about fifteen minutes.

Forty captions were generated from the roster and the results sheet. I checked
all forty against the sheet. Thirty-seven are correct.

Three are not, and one of them names a student.
```
Speaker notes: Same work. Same eleven findings behind it. The reader can act after the first sentence, and every sentence after it is support for the same decision. Count what is in that opening. An action, a named object, a number, and a time cost, all before the reader has to decide whether to keep going.

## Slide 7: The findings, the cost, and the falsifier
```
Caption 12 says a named sophomore scored the winning goal in the district
semifinal. The results sheet lists him as a manager that week. He did not
play. This is the one that cannot go to print.

Caption 31 says "the team's first district win," which the sheet does not
support either way. I could not confirm it.

What this costs: fifteen minutes and a delayed sign-off. The alternative is a
permanent printed claim about a named student that is not true.

What would change this: if the roster distinguishes managers from players and
I misread it, caption 12 may be correct.
```
Speaker notes: Three things to point at. First, the findings are quoted and each carries its evidence in one line, so a reader can check any of them in under a minute. Second, the cost is priced against the alternative, which is what makes fifteen minutes sound cheap. Third, and this is the one students resist, I could not confirm it. That sentence buys more trust than the two confident bullets above it, because it shows the confident ones were a choice rather than a habit.

## Slide 8: The rules that do most of the work
- Lead with the decision, not with the process
- Name the person, the action, and the time
- Quantify the cost. Fifteen minutes. Twelve captions
- One recommendation. Three recommendations is three briefs
- Cut the definitions. Your reader does not need them
Speaker notes: Somebody should check these and the yearbook adviser should check the twelve captions that name students before sign-off are different documents. Numbers stop a recommendation from reading as an abstraction. And say what you are not sure about, in its own sentence, because part five is what lets you be direct without overclaiming. If you have three recommendations you have three briefs, or you have one brief and everything else at the bottom under a heading that says so. For a one-page brief, the second option is nearly always right.

## Slide 9: The other wrong version, and it is subtler
- There may be some issues, and it might be worth considering
- Nothing in that sentence can be wrong
- That is why it was written, and why it is useless
- Hedging removes the risk by removing the content
- Say it definitely, and say what would change it
Speaker notes: Read that first line as though you meant it and let it hang. Nothing in it can be wrong. If the captions go to print and one of them is false, the writer can point at that sentence and say they raised it. That is what it is for. Why is it tempting? Because a definite recommendation can be wrong, in front of people, in writing. Hedging removes that risk by removing the content. The professional move is the opposite of hedging, and it is part five. Say the thing definitely, then name the specific fact that would change it.

## Slide 10: Same evidence, three readers
- The adviser needs which captions, and by when
- The student needs what to change, and that thirty-seven were fine
- The principal needs whether a student's name is at risk
- Cut the parameter names for the principal
- Changing audience is not tone. It changes paragraph one
Speaker notes: Same eleven findings, three completely different documents. For the adviser, cut anything about how models work. For the student who wrote the captions, cut the print deadline framing and lead with the thirty-seven that were right, because they have to keep working after they read it. For the principal, cut the parameter names, the method, and the other two findings, and lead with whether a student's name is at risk and what is being done about it. Changing the audience is not a matter of tone. It changes what goes in paragraph one and what comes out entirely.

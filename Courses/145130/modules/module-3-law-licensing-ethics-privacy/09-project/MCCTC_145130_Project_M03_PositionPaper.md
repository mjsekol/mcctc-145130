## The request

> From: the instructor, acting as your editor
>
> I need 1,200 words on a contested question about AI, for a reader who has to make a decision and
> does not already agree with you.
>
> Two conditions. **You argue the side you personally disagree with.** And every factual claim in
> it is one you can point at. If you cannot point at it, take it out or say plainly that you could
> not verify it.
>
> I do not want balance. Balance is what people write when they have not decided anything. I want
> the strongest version of a position, argued to somebody who starts somewhere else.
>
> Tuesday of Week 9.

**You are not being asked to change your mind and you are not being graded on whether you do.**
Several of you will finish this holding your original view more firmly and for better reasons. That
is a good outcome and it earns full marks.

## What the paper has to contain

1. **A claim.** One sentence, in the first paragraph, that somebody could disagree with.
2. **A reason.** Not a restatement of the claim in other words.
3. **Named people.** At least one group who gains and one who loses, named specifically. Not
   "society", not "users".
4. **The strongest case against your assigned position**, stated so that somebody who holds it
   would recognize it as their own view.
5. **An answer to that case**, which may be partial. "This reply is the strongest one against me
   and here is the part of it I cannot answer" is worth more than a weak refutation.
6. **What would change your mind.** One sentence, near the end, naming evidence that would count
   against the position you argued.
7. **Sources.** Every factual claim traceable. **Primary sources only for anything legal**, and
   URLs you have not loaded marked `[VERIFY]`.

## What the paper may not contain

- **A sentence describing the other side's motives.** Not what they want, not what they really
  care about. Only what they claim and why.
- **A fabricated source, statistic, case, or incident.** This is the one that ends the grade rather
  than lowering it. See the fabrication rule below.
- **A named legal case or a statute section you have not read.** The lesson on the unsettled
  question deliberately named none, for the same reason.
- **"The truth lies somewhere in the middle."** Sometimes it does. Asserting it is not analysis,
  and you saw exactly that paragraph on Thursday of Week 8.

---

## The fabrication rule

**An invented source, statistic, case, organization, or incident in this paper is not a citation
error.** It is the thing this whole course exists to train you to detect, appearing under your own
name.

If it happens, the paper comes back, you identify the fabrication yourself, and you write one
paragraph on how it got in. **Then it is graded normally.** Nobody's grade is destroyed by one
fabrication that gets fixed. What is not survivable is a fabrication you defend.

**`COULD NOT VERIFY`, with where you looked, is a full-credit way to handle a claim.** It is more
professional than a citation and it is often the more honest sentence.

## DMAIC

### Define · Week 8, Monday, Build 2 · 10 minutes

`my-actual-view.md`: what you think and why, in your own words, dated. Then the question and the
side you are arguing.

**Write this before you read anything.** It is the only evidence you will have later about what you
believed at the start, and the after-section is graded against it.

### Measure · Week 8, Tuesday and Wednesday

Read what people who hold your assigned position have written **in their own words**, not summaries
of them by people who disagree. Collect claims. For each one, record what would have to be true.

**Checkpoint.** At least four sources in `sources.md`, each with what it actually says rather than
what you want it to say.

### Analyze · Week 8, Thursday · Lab M03-02

The Steelman Exchange. Write the paragraph, get it signed off or sent back by somebody who holds
the position, rewrite it once.

**Checkpoint.** Your partner's three written answers, in your repository, marked as theirs.

### Improve · Week 8 Friday and Week 9 Monday

Write the paper around the paragraph that survived. Then run the honesty passes, in this order:

1. **The motive pass.** Read every sentence and delete any that says what the other side wants.
2. **The source pass.** Every factual claim gets a line in `sources.md` or comes out.
3. **The specificity pass.** Every "society", "people", and "users" becomes a named group or goes.
4. **The falsifier.** Write the sentence that says what would change your mind.

**Checkpoint.** Draft at 1,100 words or more, all four passes done, `sources.md` complete.

### Control · Week 9, Tuesday, Build 1

`audience.md`, the after-section of `my-actual-view.md`, and one read-through out loud. Reading it
out loud catches the hedging that reading silently does not.

**Checkpoint.** Committed and pushed by the end of Build 1.

## Scope calibration

**All three of these can score 100.**

### Too small

> A paper on question 2, arguing for detectors. It states the workload problem, says detectors help,
> acknowledges "some critics have concerns about accuracy", and concludes that schools should use
> them carefully. 1,150 words. No sources. No named groups.

**Why it is too small.** Not the length. **"Some critics have concerns" is a straw man in polite
clothing.** It names nobody, states no argument, and cannot be answered because nothing was said.
And with no sources, every factual claim in it is an assertion.

**The fix:** one paragraph stating the case against at full strength, with the error-distribution
argument in it, and four sources.

### About right

> A paper on question 4, arguing for the cloud vendor, written by a student who believes in running
> locally. It names the district's budget committee as the audience and their assumption that
> hardware is a one-time cost. It names two groups: students who get a weaker tool today, and the
> one technology coordinator who has to maintain the hardware. It states the strongest privacy case
> for local hosting, concedes that no contract fully replaces "the data never leaves the building",
> and argues that a real audit right plus a deletion clause gets most of the way there at a fraction
> of the staffing cost. It ends with the sentence: "Evidence that districts with audit rights
> actually exercise them would strengthen this. Evidence that they never do would end it."

**Why it is right.** Named audience, named assumption, named groups, a real concession, and a
falsifier that could actually come back the other way.

### Ambitious

> The same paper, plus the ideological Turing test from Lab M03-02's EXTENDED option: two
> unlabelled paragraphs, one for each side, given to two classmates who hold opposite views, with a
> write-up of who guessed what. Plus an appendix rewriting the central argument for a rights-based
> privacy audience rather than a sectoral one, and naming which argument got weaker.

**Why this is ambitious and not reckless.** Both additions test the paper rather than decorating
it.

**What would make it reckless:** adding a fifth and sixth question, or padding to 2,000 words. The
word count is a ceiling as well as a floor, and a paper that goes long usually went long because
nothing got cut.

## Grading: the standard 100-point project rubric

| Dimension | Points |
|---|---|
| Functionality | 25 |
| Code Quality | 20 |
| Documentation | 20 |
| Process | 15 |
| Demonstration | 10 |
| Polish | 10 |

**What each dimension means for this deliverable:**

- **Functionality (25).** The argument works. There is a claim, a real reason, named groups, the
  strongest case against stated fairly, an answer to it, and a falsifier. **No motive sentences.**
- **Code Quality (20).** Here this is **integrity of sourcing**. Every factual claim is traceable
  or marked `COULD NOT VERIFY` with where you looked. Primary sources for anything legal. No
  fabrications.
- **Documentation (20).** `sources.md`, `audience.md`, and `my-actual-view.md`, complete and
  honest. The audience is a real audience and not "the teacher".
- **Process (15).** Five DMAIC checkpoints on time. The partner's written answers present and
  marked as theirs. `ai-usage.md` with at least one rejection recorded.
- **Demonstration (10).** The three-minute defence below.
- **Polish (10).** 1,100 to 1,300 words. Read out loud once. No hedging paragraph. No sentence
  that could not be wrong.

## The three-minute defence

You defend the paper in Week 9, Tuesday, Build 2, to the class or to the instructor.

| Minute | What happens |
|---|---|
| 0:00 | State the question and which side you argued, and say out loud that it is the side you disagree with |
| 0:20 | Give the strongest sentence of the case you argued |
| 0:50 | Give the strongest sentence against it |
| 1:20 | **The question you will be asked:** "What is the best argument against the paper you have written, and why did you not answer it?" |
| 2:20 | Say what would change your mind |
| 2:40 | Say whether your own view moved, and if it did not, say what you learned that made it stay |

**The 1:20 question is the whole defence.** A student who cannot name the best argument against
their own paper did not steel man anything, and no amount of polish covers it.

# Instructor appendix

## The reference implementation

`reference-implementation/position-paper/` holds a completed paper on question 4, with its
`sources.md`, `audience.md`, and `my-actual-view.md`. **It argues for the cloud vendor**, which is
the side this course did not take, on purpose, because the exemplar has to model arguing against
your own institution's choice.

**Do not release it before the papers are in.** After that it is the best available example of a
falsifier sentence.

## The three ways this project goes wrong, and the intervention

**1. The straw man in polite clothing.** "Some critics have concerns." It reads balanced and it
names nothing. It is by far the most common failure and it is invisible to a spell check.

**Intervention, at the Analyze checkpoint:** ask the student to read the opposing paragraph to
somebody who holds it. Do not comment on the paragraph yourself. The partner does the work and the
student believes the partner.

**2. Sources that are summaries.** Four blog posts about what somebody argued. Zero of the thing
they argued.

**Intervention, at the Measure checkpoint:** "Which of these four is the person themselves?" If the
answer is none, send them to find one. **For anything legal, the primary source rule is absolute**,
and the reason is the module's own: this is the strand where being confidently wrong does the most
damage.

**3. The paper that cannot be wrong.** Every claim hedged, nothing committed, a conclusion that
recommends balance. It reads careful and says nothing, and students write it to avoid criticism.

**Intervention:** ask for the falsifier first, before anything else. "What evidence would count
against this?" A paper with no possible counter-evidence has no claim in it, and the student finds
that out faster from the question than from a grade.

## What to say to a student whose scope is too big

> "You are arguing three questions. Pick the one you disagree with most and cut the other two. The
> two you cut become one sentence each acknowledging that they exist. A paper that argues one thing
> well beats a paper that gestures at three."

## What to say to a student whose scope is too small

> "You have 1,150 words and no sources and no named people. Before you add anything, answer two
> questions. Who specifically gains, and who specifically loses. Then find one person who holds the
> position you are arguing and read what they wrote. The paper will double in strength without
> gaining a word."

## The fabrication protocol

If a paper contains an invented source, statistic, case, or incident:

1. Hand it back before scoring anything else.
2. **The student identifies the fabrication themselves.** Do not point at it. Tell them there is
   one and which section.
3. They write one paragraph on how it got in. The honest answer is almost always that a model
   supplied it and it looked right.
4. Then grade normally.

**Say the reason out loud:** in this course a fabrication is not a citation error, it is the thing
they are being trained to catch, appearing under their own name. **A fabrication that gets fixed
costs a student almost nothing. A fabrication that gets defended is the only outright failure in
this task.**

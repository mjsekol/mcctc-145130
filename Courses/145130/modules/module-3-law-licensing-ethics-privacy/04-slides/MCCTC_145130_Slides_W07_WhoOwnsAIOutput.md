# Who Owns the Output, and Why the Answer Is Unsettled
---
## Slide 1: Somebody is going to ask you this
- A judge, a client, an employer, a form
- Who owns the thing the model made
- Every confident one-sentence answer is wrong
- Today ends without an answer, on purpose
Speaker notes: This is the lesson with no conclusion at the bottom. That is not a gap. Anybody who gives you a confident one-sentence answer to today's question is either simplifying on purpose or does not know the state of the field. Watch for that from a model, from a teacher, and from me.

## Slide 2: Four questions, not one
- Is the output protected by copyright, and whose
- Does the output infringe somebody else's work
- Was the training on other people's work lawful
- What does the model license let you do
Speaker notes: Every argument you will hear about AI and copyright is one of these four wearing the others as a disguise. Two of them are settled enough to answer today. One is fact-specific. One is genuinely unsettled and being litigated right now.
Image: Four separated lanes, two shaded solid, one striped, one open.
---
## Slide 3: Question one, the human authorship point
- The Copyright Office protects human authorship
- Machine-generated material without it is not registrable
- Mixed works: the human part registers, the rest is disclaimed
- Other countries have taken different approaches
Speaker notes: That is the Copyright Office's stated position and you should read their registration guidance rather than my summary of it. The boundary between typing a prompt and genuinely authoring something is not a number and nobody should hand you one.
Image: A registration form with one section filled and one section disclaimed.

## Slide 4: Question three is the unsettled one
- Is training on protected works fair use
- Being litigated right now, in more than one case
- Different courts, different facts, different countries
- I will not name a case or an outcome here
Speaker notes: A case on appeal when I write this could be decided the other way by the time you read it, and a wrong citation in a lesson is worse than no citation. So you get the two strongest arguments instead, and you get them stated the way their own supporters would state them.

## Slide 5: Both sides, at full strength
- For: statistical process, new purpose, no stored copies
- For: licensing everything leaves only the largest players
- Against: a real market, and direct competition with creators
- Against: taken at a scale nobody could consent to
Speaker notes: Read both of those again tonight. If one of them looks clearly weaker to you, you have not understood it yet, and your position paper next week is going to be thin. Your job is to write the side you disagree with well enough that somebody who holds it would sign your paragraph.

## Slide 6: Question four takes four minutes
```
7. OUTPUT. Lantern Research Collective claims no ownership in material you
   generate with the Model Weights. This Section is a statement about what
   Lantern Research Collective will and will not claim under this contract. It
   is not a statement about whether any law gives you or anyone else a copyright
   in that material.
```
Speaker notes: That is section seven of the model license in your fixture. The second half is written out loud because the real ones usually leave it implied and readers fill it in wrongly. Read that clause twice.

## Slide 7: Two claims in one clause
- We will not assert ownership: a contract promise
- You hold a copyright: a statement about the law
- The license makes the first and not the second
- No contract can create a copyright the law withholds
Speaker notes: This is the most common confusion in the whole subject. A promise not to sue you is not a grant of copyright. When terms of service say you own the output, they are telling you what the company will do. Whether anybody holds a copyright is question one, and the terms do not decide it.
Image: One clause split into two boxes, one labelled contract, one labelled law.

## Slide 8: You cannot compute ownership. You can record authorship
```
human                      860 words   58.8%
human_edit_of_model        280 words   19.2%
model                      322 words   22.0%

Total words: 1462
Sections whose first draft came from a model: 3
```
Speaker notes: Real percentages from a real provenance record. Look at the row with zero words that is not on this screen: the data flow diagram, human work this program cannot count because it counts words. A metric that cannot see part of the work always understates that part.

## Slide 9: The wrong version, written by a model
- All assets are original. The images were AI generated
- Which means nobody else holds copyright
- So we own them outright
- Our submission is fully original work
Speaker notes: Four mistakes in three sentences, and a judge is going to read it. Original is doing two different jobs. Not copyrighted by anybody else does not follow from generated with AI. So we own it does not follow from either. And fully original hides the disclosure inside a sentence that concludes there is nothing to disclose.
Image: A submission form paragraph with four separate underlines.
---
## Slide 10: The honest version is shorter and stronger
- Drafted with a local model, then edited by us
- Our provenance record lists which sections
- We are not claiming copyright in the unedited output
- Third-party components are listed with their licenses
Speaker notes: Saying this is unsettled, here are the facts it turns on, here is what we did about it, is the strongest answer available in this course and in any professional setting. A confident wrong answer is the weak one. Next week you will be graded on telling those two apart.
Image: A four-line disclosure paragraph, clean, in navy.

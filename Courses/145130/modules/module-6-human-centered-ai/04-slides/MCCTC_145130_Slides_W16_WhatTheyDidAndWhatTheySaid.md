# What a user did, and what a user said
---
## Slide 1: They said it was fine. It took them three minutes.
- Expected twenty seconds
- One error, one trip to help
- Ended with "yeah, that was no problem"
- Both of those are true at the same time
Speaker notes: Somebody sat down with a program last week and took two minutes and forty-eight seconds on a task the person who built it expected to take twenty seconds. They hit an error. They went and read the help. And at the end they said, yeah that was no problem. Both of those things are true, and only one of them is something you can do anything about on Monday morning.

## Slide 2: You cannot unknow your own program
- You know where the files are
- You know what every word means
- That knowledge is permanent now
- The only way out is watching somebody else
Speaker notes: You have used this thing for three weeks. You know which flag it needs and what every word in the output means. There is no way to think your way back to not knowing that, which means there is no way to work out what a stranger will get stuck on by sitting at your desk. The only instrument is a person who has never seen it.
Image: Two overlapping circles, what you know and what they know, with a small sliver in common.

## Slide 3: Two columns. That is the whole method.
```
WHAT THEY DID                          WHAT THEY SAID
opened the folder, not the program     "where is it"
read the README for 40 s               "ok this makes sense"
typed the command with no --file       "did I break it"
retyped it with --file                 "that was fine"
```
Speaker notes: This is a real record from a real session. Left column is what happened, with times. Right column is what came out of their mouth. Look at the bottom row of each column. That was fine, from a person who needed two attempts and forty seconds of reading. Neither column is wrong. They are answering different questions.

## Slide 4: When they disagree, behaviour wins
- Your program is used by people doing things
- Not by people answering questions about it
- Count the first, record the second
- Mark the second as an opinion
Speaker notes: When the two columns disagree, and they will disagree constantly, the left one wins. Your program gets used by somebody trying to get something done at eight in the morning, not by somebody sitting calmly giving you feedback. You count what they did. You write down what they said and you label it an opinion, because that is what it is.
Image: A balance scale with a stopwatch on one side outweighing a speech bubble on the other.

## Slide 5: Three numbers, and all three are free
```
time on task    start to having the answer
attempts        how many tries before something worked
where first     the first place they looked, right or wrong
```
Speaker notes: Three numbers you can collect with a pen. Time on task turns it was fine into it took four times as long as you expected. Attempts is the one that finds the startup problem every single time. And where they looked first tells you what your program looks like from outside, which you will never guess correctly.

## Slide 6: The wrong way, and what it costs
- "Run it with the --file flag, then open digest.md"
- "Did that work?"
- "What did you think?"
- Fifteen minutes spent, one note about colours
Speaker notes: Here is the wrong version and it is what nearly everybody does first. You tell them the command. You ask did that work, which tells them something was supposed to work. Then you ask what they thought, which invites a review. Fifteen minutes of a real person's time, and what you have is a suggestion about colours from somebody who was never allowed to get lost.
Image: A session sheet with one line written on it, mostly blank, accent red border.

## Slide 7: You will want to help at twenty seconds
- The silence feels like a minute
- They are doing you a favour
- Letting them struggle is the favour you asked for
- Say so at the start, then be quiet
Speaker notes: Twenty seconds of somebody hunting through the wrong folder feels like a minute, and you will want to help. Everybody does, including people who have done this for years. That is why the rule is written down instead of left to judgement. You told them at the start that you were testing the program and not them. Being quiet is you keeping that promise.
Image: A person watching a screen with their hands deliberately in their lap.

## Slide 8: Five rules, and none of them is optional
- They say yes out loud, after the consent line
- No recording of any kind
- No names. Participants are P1 to P5
- No classmates in the formal study
Speaker notes: Five rules. They say yes out loud after you read the consent line. You record nothing, no audio, no video, no screenshots. You write no names, so participants are P1 through P5. No classmates in the formal study, because they know what you want to hear. And rule five, nothing from a session goes into an AI tool, ever, including a summary of it at eleven at night.
Image: A short numbered card with five rules, the fifth one highlighted in accent red.

## Slide 9: The consent line, word for word
```
I am testing a program I built, and I am not testing you.
Anything that goes wrong is information I need.
I will ask you to do a few things and then stay quiet while
you do them, and that is on purpose rather than rude.
I am not recording anything and I am not writing your name.
You can stop at any point and you do not have to say why.
Is that okay?
```
Speaker notes: You read this as written. You do not paraphrase it, because the bits that get lost in a paraphrase are the recording line and the stopping line, and those are the two that matter. Then you wait for an answer out loud. A nod is not a yes.

## Slide 10: What you are about to build
- A rehearsal: watch a classmate use your program
- Two columns, six rows, times on three
- A list of five real people and a time each
- One sentence saying what your study is for
Speaker notes: Build one is a rehearsal with somebody across the room. Not data, because they are classmates, but the only safe place to feel the silence. Build two is finding your five people, and I want thirty minutes on it, because a team that leaves this to Wednesday does not have five people on Friday.
